Unit 1 · Atomic models 16 cards
The five models in order?›

Dalton (solid sphere) → Thomson (plum pudding) → Rutherford (nucleus) → Bohr (energy levels) → quantum mechanical (electron cloud).

Three observations of the gold foil experiment, with conclusions?›

Most went straight through: mostly empty space. A few were deflected: positive charge is concentrated. Very few bounced back: the nucleus is tiny, dense and positive.

Orbit vs orbital?›

Orbit (Bohr): a fixed circular path. Orbital (quantum): a region where an electron is probably found.

Who discovered the electron?›

Thomson. Thomson's cathode ray tube (1897): the ray bent toward a positive plate, so it was made of negative particles.

Which experiment showed that atoms have a small, dense, positive nucleus?›

The gold foil (alpha scattering) experiment. Rutherford, 1911. A few alpha particles bounced back, so something tiny, dense and positive had to be in the way.

Thomson's model of the atom is known as the›

plum pudding model. Electrons embedded in a sphere of positive charge, like fruit in a pudding.

In the gold foil experiment, what did the few alpha particles that bounced straight back show?›

The positive charge and most of the mass are packed into a tiny nucleus. Only a head-on hit with something small, dense and positive could turn an alpha particle around.

Most alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil because›

most of the atom is empty space. They hit nothing. The nucleus is a tiny target.

What did Millikan's oil drop experiment measure?›

The charge of an electron. It gave the charge of one electron, which with Thomson's result also gave the electron's mass.

Which is a limitation of Rutherford's nuclear model?›

It cannot explain why orbiting electrons do not lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. It also could not explain line spectra. Bohr's fixed energy levels answered both.

What evidence did Bohr use to propose fixed energy levels?›

The line spectrum of hydrogen. Only certain colors appear, so only certain energy changes are possible, so the levels must be fixed.

Why did Bohr's model have to be replaced?›

It only works for atoms with one electron, such as hydrogen. It fails for the spectra of larger atoms, and electrons do not really follow neat circular paths.

Who discovered the neutron?›

Chadwick. Chadwick, 1932, by bombarding beryllium with alpha particles.

In the quantum mechanical model, an orbital is›

a region where an electron is likely to be found. An orbital is a probability region (electron cloud). A fixed circular path is a Bohr orbit.

Which part of Dalton's atomic theory is no longer accepted?›

Atoms cannot be divided. Atoms are made of protons, neutrons and electrons. Isotopes also show that atoms of one element are not all identical.

Which list puts the models in order from oldest to newest?›

Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, quantum. Ball, pudding, nucleus, levels, cloud.

Unit 1 · Subatomic particles 10 cards
Charge, mass and location of the three particles?›

Proton: +1, 1 amu, nucleus. Neutron: 0, 1 amu, nucleus. Electron: −1, about 1/1836 amu, electron cloud.

The three counting rules?›

Protons = atomic number. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number. Electrons = protons − charge.

Which particle determines the identity of an element?›

The proton. The number of protons is the atomic number. Change it and the element changes.

Which particles are found in the nucleus?›

Protons and neutrons. Electrons are outside the nucleus, in the electron cloud.

What is the relative charge of a neutron?›

0. Neutral. Proton +1, electron −1.

Which particle has the smallest mass?›

The electron. An electron has about 1/1836 the mass of a proton.

The mass number of an atom equals›

protons + neutrons. Electrons are too light to count. Neutrons = mass number − atomic number.

Where is most of the mass of an atom?›

In the nucleus. Protons and neutrons are in the nucleus, and each is about 1836 times heavier than an electron.

Compared with the whole atom, the nucleus is›

tiny, but it holds nearly all the mass. The nucleus is about 100 000 times smaller than the atom. Most of the atom is empty space.

Protons and neutrons together are called›

nucleons. Both live in the nucleus.

Unit 1 · Isotopes vs ions 10 cards
Isotope vs ion in one line each?›

Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. Ions: same protons, different electrons.

How do you name ions?›

Positive ion: element name + "ion" (sodium ion). Negative ion: ending changes to -ide (chloride ion).

Isotopes of an element differ in their number of›

neutrons. Same protons (same element), different neutrons, so a different mass number.

An atom becomes an ion by gaining or losing›

electrons. The nucleus does not change. Only the electron count does.

Which pair are isotopes?›

¹²₆C and ¹⁴₆C. Same protons (6), different neutrons (6 and 8). The second pair are different elements; the last two are an atom and its ion.

A positively charged ion is called›

a cation. Cation = positive (lost electrons). Anion = negative (gained electrons).

What is the name of Cl⁻?›

Chloride ion. A single-atom negative ion ends in -ide.

What is the name of Na⁺?›

Sodium ion. A positive ion keeps the element's name. Sodium has only one charge, so no Roman numeral.

A neutral atom gains two electrons. Its charge becomes›

2−. Electrons are negative, so gaining two makes the charge 2−.

Why do isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties?›

They have the same number of protons and electrons. Chemistry is decided by the electrons. Neutrons only change the mass.

Unit 1 · Average atomic mass 7 cards
Average atomic mass formula?›

Sum of (isotope mass × fractional abundance). Fraction = percent ÷ 100, and the fractions add to 1.

Two isotopes, unknown percents: the set-up?›

Let x and (1 − x) be the fractions. mass₁·x + mass₂·(1 − x) = average. Solve for x.

The atomic mass on the periodic table is›

the weighted average of the masses of the element's natural isotopes. Each isotope counts in proportion to its abundance.

Neon has isotopes Ne-20, Ne-21 and Ne-22 and an average atomic mass of 20.18 amu. Which isotope is most abundant?›

Ne-20. The average is always closest to the most abundant isotope.

Before using a percent abundance in the formula, you must›

divide it by 100 to get a decimal fraction. 75.78% becomes 0.7578.

An element has two isotopes. If the fraction of one is x, the fraction of the other is›

1 − x. The fractions must add up to 1.

Why is the atomic mass of chlorine (35.45 amu) not a whole number?›

It is a weighted average of isotopes with different masses. No single chlorine atom has a mass of 35.45. It is the average over the natural mixture.

Unit 1 · Nuclear fusion 11 cards
Fusion vs fission?›

Fusion: light nuclei join (Sun, stars); needs extreme temperature and pressure. Fission: a heavy nucleus splits (power plants). Both release huge energy and form new elements.

How do you balance a nuclear equation?›

The mass numbers (top) add to the same total on both sides, and so do the atomic numbers (bottom).

Nuclear fusion is›

two light nuclei combining to form a heavier nucleus. Fusion joins. Fission splits.

Where does fusion happen naturally?›

In the Sun and other stars. Stars fuse hydrogen into helium. Power plants use fission.

Why does fusion need an extremely high temperature?›

Nuclei are positive and repel, so they must move very fast to get close enough to fuse. Like charges repel. Only very fast (very hot) nuclei collide hard enough to join.

In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei fuse to form›

helium. Hydrogen fuses to helium and releases the energy that makes the Sun shine.

In a balanced nuclear equation, which totals must match on both sides?›

The mass numbers and the atomic numbers. Add the top numbers and the bottom numbers separately on each side.

Where does the energy released in fusion come from?›

A small amount of mass is converted into energy. The products have slightly less mass than the reactants. E = mc².

How is a nuclear reaction different from a chemical reaction?›

The nucleus changes, so a new element forms. Chemical reactions only rearrange electrons, and the elements stay the same.

Nuclear fission is›

a heavy nucleus splitting into smaller nuclei. For example, uranium-235 splitting when a neutron hits it.

Compared with chemical reactions, nuclear reactions release›

far more energy. Millions of times more energy for the same mass of fuel.

Unit 1 · Electron configuration 13 cards
Filling order up to 4p?›

1s → 2s → 2p → 3s → 3p → 4s → 3d → 4p. Capacities: s 2, p 6, d 10, f 14.

The three rules?›

Aufbau: lowest energy first. Pauli: at most 2 electrons per orbital, opposite spins. Hund: one electron in each orbital of a sublevel before pairing.

How do you spot an excited state?›

Count the electrons to find the element, then compare with the filling order. An electron in a higher sublevel while a lower one is not full means excited.

How do you write a noble gas configuration?›

Put the previous noble gas (end of the row above) in brackets, then write only the electrons after it. [He] 2, [Ne] 10, [Ar] 18, [Kr] 36.

What is the maximum number of electrons in one orbital?›

2. Two electrons with opposite spins (Pauli exclusion principle).

What is the maximum number of electrons in a p sublevel?›

6. Three orbitals × 2 electrons. s holds 2, d holds 10, f holds 14.

What is the maximum number of electrons in a d sublevel?›

10. Five orbitals × 2 electrons.

Which sublevel fills straight after 3p?›

4s. 4s is lower in energy than 3d. There is no 3f.

Hund's rule says that›

each orbital in a sublevel gets one electron before any orbital gets two. One in each box first, all with the same spin. Then pair.

The Pauli exclusion principle says that›

an orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins. One up arrow and one down arrow per box, never two the same.

The Aufbau principle says that›

electrons fill the lowest-energy sublevel first. Build up from 1s, following the diagonal rule.

What is the ground-state configuration of chromium?›

[Ar] 4s¹ 3d⁵. An exception: a half-filled 3d sublevel is extra stable. Copper is the other one: [Ar] 4s¹ 3d¹⁰.

Compared with its ground state, an atom in an excited state has›

the same number of electrons but more energy. An electron has moved to a higher level. Nothing was added or removed.

Unit 1 · Light emission 14 cards
Excitation and de-excitation?›

Excitation: the electron absorbs energy and jumps up. De-excitation: it falls back and emits a photon with energy equal to the gap between the levels.

How are energy, frequency and wavelength linked?›

E = hf and c = λf. Higher energy means higher frequency and shorter wavelength. Violet is high energy; red is low.

What happens when an electron in an atom absorbs energy?›

It jumps to a higher energy level. This is excitation. The atom is now in an excited state.

When does an atom emit light?›

When an electron falls from a higher level to a lower level. This is de-excitation. The energy leaves as a photon.

The energy of the photon emitted equals›

the difference in energy between the two levels. E(photon) = E(high) − E(low).

Which color of visible light has the highest energy?›

Violet. Violet has the shortest wavelength and the highest frequency.

As the wavelength of light decreases, its frequency›

increases. c = λf and c is constant, so they change in opposite directions.

As the frequency of light increases, the energy of its photons›

increases. E = hf.

An atom whose electrons are all in the lowest possible energy levels is in›

the ground state. Any higher arrangement is an excited state.

A bigger energy drop inside an atom emits light with›

a shorter wavelength. More energy means higher frequency and shorter wavelength.

Why does a glowing gas give a line spectrum and not a rainbow?›

Energy levels are fixed, so only certain energy changes are possible. Quantized levels give only certain photon energies, so only certain colors.

In a flame test, the color is produced when›

excited electrons fall back to lower energy levels. The flame excites the electrons. The color comes out on the way back down.

Which color of visible light has the longest wavelength?›

Red. Red is about 700 nm, violet about 400 nm.

An excited state is›

unstable: the electron soon falls back and emits light. Atoms return to the ground state almost at once.

Unit 1 · Emission spectra 11 cards
The matching rule for emission spectra?›

An element is present only if every one of its lines appears in the unknown. Leftover lines belong to another element.

Hydrogen's four visible lines?›

410 nm (violet), 434 nm (blue-violet), 486 nm (blue-green), 656 nm (red). All are drops to n = 2.

Why is an emission spectrum called an element's fingerprint?›

Each element has its own unique set of lines. Different elements have different energy levels, so different lines.

When can you say an element is present in an unknown sample?›

When all of the element's lines appear in the unknown's spectrum. Every line must match. One match is not enough.

An unknown spectrum shows every line of hydrogen plus some extra lines. What can you conclude?›

Hydrogen is present, with at least one other element. The extra lines must come from something else.

A continuous spectrum contains›

all the colors with no gaps. White light through a prism gives a continuous spectrum.

A line emission spectrum is produced by›

a glowing (excited) gas of an element. Excited atoms emit only their own particular colors.

Each line in an emission spectrum corresponds to›

one particular electron transition (energy drop). Each drop between two levels gives photons of one energy, so one line.

Why do different elements give different spectra?›

Their energy levels are different. Different nuclear charge and electron arrangement give different level spacings.

The visible lines of hydrogen come from electrons dropping to›

n = 2. Drops to n = 1 are ultraviolet and drops to n = 3 are infrared.

How do astronomers know which elements are in a star?›

They match the lines in its spectrum with those of known elements. The same matching rule you use on the exam.

Unit 2 · Atomic and ionic radius 10 cards
Atomic radius trends and the reason?›

Decreases across a period (more protons, same shielding). Increases down a group (more energy levels, more shielding).

Ionic radius rules?›

Cation smaller than its atom; anion larger. For ions with the same number of electrons, more protons means smaller.

Across a period from left to right, atomic radius generally›

decreases. More protons pull the same energy level in closer.

Down a group, atomic radius›

increases. Each row adds an energy level.

Why does atomic radius decrease across a period?›

More protons pull the electrons in the same energy level closer. Nuclear charge rises while shielding stays about the same.

Why does atomic radius increase down a group?›

Each row adds an energy level, so the outer electrons are farther away and more shielded. Distance and shielding outweigh the extra protons.

Compared with its neutral atom, a cation is›

smaller. It has lost electrons, often a whole energy level, and the same protons pull on fewer electrons.

Compared with its neutral atom, an anion is›

larger. Extra electrons repel each other, and the same protons hold more electrons.

Na⁺ and F⁻ both have 10 electrons. Why is Na⁺ smaller?›

It has more protons pulling on the same number of electrons. 11 protons against 9, for the same 10 electrons.

What is shielding?›

Inner electrons reducing the pull of the nucleus on the outer electrons. Core electrons sit between the nucleus and the valence electrons.

Unit 2 · Ionization energy and electronegativity 14 cards
Ionization energy and electronegativity trends?›

Both increase across a period and decrease down a group. F is the most electronegative; He has the highest ionization energy.

How do you write a trend explanation?›

Claim → cause (protons, shielding or energy levels) → effect (how strongly the outer electrons are held).

What does a big jump in successive ionization energies tell you?›

The jump comes when a core electron is removed, so the number of electrons before the jump is the number of valence electrons.

First ionization energy is the energy needed to›

remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom. The more tightly the electron is held, the higher the ionization energy.

Electronegativity is›

how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a bond. It only applies to atoms in a bond.

Across a period, first ionization energy generally›

increases. Greater nuclear charge with the same shielding holds the electrons more tightly.

Down a group, first ionization energy›

decreases. The outer electron is farther away and more shielded.

Which element has the highest electronegativity?›

F. Top right of the table (ignoring the noble gases).

Why does ionization energy decrease down a group?›

The outer electron is farther from the nucleus and more shielded, so it is held less tightly. Distance and shielding win over the larger nuclear charge.

Why does ionization energy increase across a period?›

Nuclear charge increases while shielding stays about the same. The outer electrons feel a stronger pull, so more energy is needed to remove one.

A small atom usually has›

a high ionization energy and a high electronegativity. Small atom, tight hold on electrons.

Metals tend to have›

low ionization energies, so they form cations. They lose electrons easily.

Which element has the highest first ionization energy of all?›

He. Helium is the smallest atom with a full level and no shielding.

A large difference in electronegativity between two atoms gives›

an ionic bond. Above about 1.7 the electron is transferred. This is the link between Unit 2 and Unit 3.

Unit 3 · Chemical bonding 109 cards
The ΔEN rule for bond type?›

Less than 0.5: nonpolar covalent. 0.5 to 1.7: polar covalent. More than 1.7: ionic. Metal + metal is metallic.

Naming rules in one card?›

Metal first: ionic, no prefixes, Roman numeral if the metal has more than one charge, polyatomic ions keep their names. Two non-metals: prefixes and -ide.

When is a molecule polar?›

When it has polar bonds and the dipoles do not cancel: a lone pair on the central atom, or outer atoms that differ.

Which type of bond holds NaCl together?›

Ionic. A metal (Na) with a non-metal (Cl): ΔEN = 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1, so electrons are transferred and the ions attract.

Which type of bond holds a piece of copper wire together?›

Metallic. Copper is a metal: its + ions sit in a sea of free-moving electrons.

Which type of bond is in a Cl₂ molecule?›

Nonpolar covalent. Two identical non-metal atoms: ΔEN = 0, so the pair is shared equally.

The O–H bond in water has ΔEN = 1.4. What kind of bond is it?›

Polar covalent. Two non-metals with ΔEN between 0.5 and 1.7: shared unequally, pulled toward O.

In an ionic bond, what happens to the electrons?›

They are transferred from the metal to the non-metal. The metal loses electrons and becomes a + ion; the non-metal gains them and becomes a − ion. The opposite charges attract.

In a metallic bond, what do the valence electrons do?›

They move freely among the positive metal ions. This "sea of electrons" explains why metals conduct and can be bent.

A C–H bond has ΔEN = 0.4. What kind of bond is it?›

Nonpolar covalent. Below 0.5 counts as nonpolar covalent.

A metal and a non-metal have ΔEN = 2.3. What kind of bond forms?›

Ionic. Above about 1.7 between a metal and a non-metal, the electron is transferred: ionic.

Brass is made of copper and zinc. What holds it together?›

Metallic bonds. Two metals: an alloy, held by metallic bonding.

In the polar bond H–Cl, which atom is δ− (slightly negative)?›

Cl, because it is more electronegative. The shared pair is pulled toward the more electronegative atom, Cl (3.0 vs 2.1).

Sodium nitrate, NaNO₃, contains which bonds?›

Both ionic and covalent. Ionic between Na⁺ and NO₃⁻; covalent between N and O inside the nitrate ion.

What is the difference between a polar and a nonpolar covalent bond?›

Whether the shared electrons are shared unequally or equally. Both share electrons. In a polar bond one atom pulls the pair closer.

What does ΔEN stand for?›

The difference in electronegativity between the two bonded atoms. ΔEN = larger EN − smaller EN. Under 0.5 nonpolar, 0.5–1.7 polar covalent, over 1.7 ionic.

In the dot diagram of CaCl₂, what do you draw?›

One [Ca]²⁺ and two [Cl]⁻, each Cl with 8 dots. Ca gives away 2 electrons, one to each Cl. Charges: 2+ and 2 × 1− add to zero.

How many valence electrons does CO₃²⁻ have in total?›

24. C 4 + O 3 × 6 = 22, plus 2 for the 2− charge = 24.

How many valence electrons does NH₄⁺ have in total?›

8. N 5 + H 4 × 1 = 9, minus 1 for the + charge = 8.

Which atom goes in the center of the Lewis structure of CH₂O?›

C. Carbon forms the most bonds (4) and is the least electronegative. H is never central.

Why can hydrogen never be the central atom?›

It can form only one bond. H needs only 2 electrons, so it makes one bond and is always on the outside.

How many lone pairs are on the O atom in H₂O?›

2. 8 electrons: 2 O–H bonds use 4, the other 4 are 2 lone pairs on O.

What kind of bond joins the two atoms in N₂?›

A triple bond. 10 electrons: N≡N with one lone pair on each N gives both atoms 8.

How many electrons are in a double bond?›

4. Each dash is one shared pair = 2 electrons. A double bond is 2 pairs.

What must the Lewis structure of a polyatomic ion always show?›

Square brackets with the charge outside. The charge belongs to the whole ion, so it goes outside the brackets, top right.

NO₃⁻ is drawn as three structures joined by ↔. What does that mean?›

The double bond can go to any of the three O atoms; the real ion is a blend. That is resonance. All three N–O bonds are identical in the real ion.

What is the formal charge on N in NH₄⁺?›

+1. FC = valence − lone-pair electrons − bonds = 5 − 0 − 4 = +1.

Which carbon–carbon bond is the shortest and strongest?›

C≡C. Higher bond order = more shared pairs = shorter and stronger.

In step 4 of drawing a Lewis structure, the central atom has only 6 electrons. What do you do?›

Move a lone pair from an outer atom to make a double bond. You never add electrons. You turn a lone pair into a shared pair.

Which type of substance conducts electricity as a solid?›

Metallic. Only metals have electrons that are free to move in the solid.

Solid NaCl does not conduct electricity, but melted NaCl does. Why?›

The ions are free to move once it melts. Ions carry the current. In the solid they are locked in the lattice.

Why are ionic crystals brittle?›

A shifted layer puts like charges next to each other, and they repel. A knock moves one layer by one ion: + meets + and − meets −, so the crystal splits.

Why can metals be hammered into sheets without breaking?›

The ion layers slide while the electron sea keeps holding them. Malleable and ductile: nothing has to break when the layers move.

Sugar is covalent and melts at a low temperature. Why?›

The forces between its molecules are weak. Melting separates molecules from each other. It does not break the bonds inside them.

A solid is hard, brittle, melts at 800 °C and conducts only when dissolved in water. What is it?›

Ionic. High melting point, brittle, conducts only when the ions can move: ionic.

A solid is shiny, bends without breaking and conducts electricity. What is it?›

Metallic. Lustre, malleability and conduction as a solid all come from the electron sea.

A substance is a gas at room temperature and does not conduct. What is it?›

Molecular covalent. Small molecules with weak forces between them: low melting and boiling points.

A solid is extremely hard, melts above 3000 °C and does not conduct or dissolve. What is it?›

Covalent network. Every atom is joined to its neighbors by covalent bonds, as in diamond.

As bond order goes up (single → double → triple), what happens?›

The bond gets shorter and stronger. More shared pairs pull the two nuclei closer and take more energy to break.

Water and methane have molecules of similar size, but water boils far higher. Why?›

Water molecules are polar, so they attract each other strongly. Polar molecules attract each other; nonpolar ones have only weak attractions.

What are ionic compounds made of?›

A repeating lattice of + and − ions. There is no "NaCl molecule": each Na⁺ is surrounded by Cl⁻ and each Cl⁻ by Na⁺.

What is the name of N₂O₅?›

Dinitrogen pentoxide. Two non-metals: prefixes. The "a" of penta- is dropped before "oxide".

What is the name of CO?›

Carbon monoxide. No mono- on the first element, but the second always takes a prefix.

What is the name of MgCl₂?›

Magnesium chloride. Metal + non-metal: no prefixes. Mg only forms 2+, so no Roman numeral either.

What is the name of FeCl₃?›

Iron(III) chloride. Three Cl⁻ make 3−, so the iron is Fe³⁺. Iron has more than one charge, so the numeral is needed.

What is the name of CuO?›

Copper(II) oxide. O is 2−, so the single copper ion must be Cu²⁺.

What is the name of Cu₂O?›

Copper(I) oxide. O is 2−, shared by two copper ions, so each is Cu⁺.

What is the name of NaNO₃?›

Sodium nitrate. NO₃⁻ is the polyatomic ion nitrate. Use its own name.

What is the name of CaCO₃?›

Calcium carbonate. CO₃²⁻ is carbonate. Calcium only forms 2+, so no numeral.

What is the name of (NH₄)₂SO₄?›

Ammonium sulfate. Two polyatomic ions: ammonium NH₄⁺ and sulfate SO₄²⁻. No prefixes in ionic names.

What is the name of PCl₃?›

Phosphorus trichloride. Two non-metals: prefixes, with no mono- on the first element.

What is the name of SF₆?›

Sulfur hexafluoride. Two non-metals: hexa- for 6.

What is the formula of aluminum oxide?›

Al₂O₃. Al³⁺ and O²⁻: criss-cross the charge numbers to get Al₂O₃ (6+ and 6−).

What is the formula of calcium hydroxide?›

Ca(OH)₂. Ca²⁺ needs two OH⁻. Two of the same polyatomic ion go in parentheses.

What is the formula of iron(II) sulfate?›

FeSO₄. Fe²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ balance one to one.

What is the formula of dinitrogen tetroxide?›

N₂O₄. Read the prefixes as subscripts: di = 2, tetra = 4.

When do you use prefixes such as di- and tri- in a name?›

When both elements are non-metals (a covalent compound). Ionic names never use prefixes: the charges fix the ratio.

When does a name need a Roman numeral?›

When the metal can form more than one charge. Iron, copper and most transition metals. Not Na, Ca, Al, Zn or Ag.

What does the (III) in iron(III) oxide tell you?›

Each iron ion has a 3+ charge. The numeral is the charge of the metal ion. The formula is Fe₂O₃.

What is the name of the ion SO₃²⁻?›

Sulfite. -ite has one oxygen fewer than -ate: sulfate is SO₄²⁻. Sulfide is S²⁻.

What is the name of H₂S?›

Hydrogen sulfide. When hydrogen comes first it takes no prefix.

What is the formula of copper(II) nitrate?›

Cu(NO₃)₂. Cu²⁺ needs two NO₃⁻, in parentheses.

Which material is best for the core of an electrical cable, and why?›

Copper: its free electrons carry current and it can be drawn into wire. Metallic bonding gives conduction in the solid and ductility.

Which material is best for the coating around an electrical wire?›

A plastic (molecular covalent): it insulates and bends. Molecular covalent materials have no free charges, so they insulate, and they are flexible.

Which material is best for lining a furnace?›

An ionic ceramic: very high melting point. Strong attraction between the ions means it survives very high temperatures.

Which material is best for the tip of a drill that cuts rock?›

Diamond (covalent network): the hardest. In a covalent network every atom is locked in by covalent bonds.

Why is an alloy usually harder than the pure metal?›

Atoms of a different size stop the layers sliding easily. Sliding layers are what make a pure metal soft. Disrupting them makes it harder and stronger.

What is brass made of?›

Copper and zinc. Brass = Cu + Zn. Bronze = Cu + Sn.

What is bronze made of?›

Copper and tin. Bronze = Cu + Sn. Brass = Cu + Zn.

Which element added to steel makes stainless steel resist rust?›

Chromium. Stainless steel is iron + carbon + chromium + nickel.

Why would an ionic ceramic make a bad hammer head?›

It is brittle and would shatter. Ionic materials are hard but brittle: fine for a tile, wrong for anything that takes impacts.

Why are frying-pan handles made of plastic or wood, not metal?›

They are poor conductors of heat. The metal body conducts heat to the food; the covalent handle keeps the heat from your hand.

You need a coating that water will not stick to. Which should you choose?›

A nonpolar covalent material. Water is polar. Nonpolar materials such as wax or nonstick coatings do not attract it.

An alloy is best described as…›

A homogeneous mixture of a metal with other elements. No new compound forms: the atoms are mixed, still held by metallic bonding.

You need a substance that conducts electricity when dissolved in water. Which type?›

Ionic. Dissolving frees the ions, and moving ions carry the current.

Which property makes metals good for cooking pots?›

They conduct heat well. The free electrons carry heat as well as charge.

A watch face must be transparent, very hard and able to stand heat. Which type of material fits?›

Covalent network, such as quartz (SiO₂). Network solids are hard with very high melting points, and many are transparent.

What is the first step in choosing a material for a job?›

List the properties the job needs. Then match those properties to the bond type that gives them.

How many electron domains are around C in CO₂?›

2. Two double bonds, and each counts as one domain.

What is the molecular geometry of CH₄?›

Tetrahedral. 4 bonded atoms and no lone pairs on C.

What is the molecular geometry of NH₃?›

Trigonal pyramidal. 4 domains: 3 atoms + 1 lone pair.

What is the molecular geometry of H₂O?›

Bent. 4 domains: 2 atoms + 2 lone pairs.

What is the molecular geometry of CO₂?›

Linear. 2 domains and no lone pairs on C.

What is the molecular geometry of BF₃?›

Trigonal planar. 3 domains and no lone pair on B.

What is the molecular geometry of SO₂?›

Bent. 3 domains: 2 atoms + 1 lone pair on S.

What is the bond angle in CH₄?›

109.5°. Four domains point to the corners of a tetrahedron.

What is the bond angle in H₂O?›

About 104.5°. Two lone pairs squeeze the angle below 109.5°.

What is the bond angle in BF₃?›

120°. Three domains spread evenly in a plane.

What is the ELECTRON geometry of NH₃?›

Tetrahedral. Electron geometry counts all 4 domains, lone pair included.

What is the ELECTRON geometry of H₂O?›

Tetrahedral. 2 bonded atoms + 2 lone pairs = 4 domains.

A central atom has 3 electron domains, one of them a lone pair. What is the shape?›

Bent, a little less than 120°. Only 2 atoms are attached, so the atoms form a bent shape.

Why is the bond angle in NH₃ (107°) smaller than in CH₄ (109.5°)?›

The lone pair repels more than a bonding pair. Lone pairs take more space and push the bonds closer together.

What does a solid wedge mean in a 3-D drawing?›

The bond comes out of the page, toward you. Plain = in the page, wedge = toward you, dash = away.

What is the molecular geometry of the nitrate ion, NO₃⁻?›

Trigonal planar. N has 3 bonded O atoms and no lone pair: 3 domains.

What is the molecular geometry of PCl₃?›

Trigonal pyramidal. 26 electrons: P has 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 domains.

CO₂ has two polar C=O bonds. Why is the molecule nonpolar?›

It is linear, so the two equal dipoles cancel. Two equal arrows pointing in opposite directions add to zero.

Why is H₂O a polar molecule?›

It is bent, so the two O–H dipoles add instead of cancelling. The shape decides: the two arrows point partly the same way.

Is a C–H bond polar? (EN: C 2.5, H 2.1)›

No: ΔEN is 0.4. A bond with ΔEN below 0.5 counts as nonpolar.

In an O–H bond, which atom is δ−?›

O. O is more electronegative (3.5 vs 2.1), so it pulls the shared pair.

What is true of CCl₄?›

Its bonds are polar but the molecule is nonpolar. C–Cl is polar (ΔEN 0.5). Four equal dipoles in a tetrahedron cancel.

What is true of CH₄?›

Its bonds are nonpolar and the molecule is nonpolar. C–H has ΔEN 0.4: no polar bonds, so nothing to add up.

CF₂Cl₂ is tetrahedral. Is it polar?›

Yes: the outer atoms are not all the same. The C–F dipoles are stronger than the C–Cl dipoles, so they cannot cancel.

HCN is linear. Is it polar?›

Yes: the two ends are different. Linear cancels only when both outer atoms are the same, as in CO₂.

A bond dipole arrow points toward…›

The more electronegative atom. That is where the shared electrons are pulled: the δ− end.

A molecule has polar bonds and a lone pair on its central atom (bent or pyramidal). It is…›

Polar. The lone pair makes the shape unsymmetric, so the dipoles do not cancel.

BF₃ or NF₃: which one is polar?›

NF₃: it has a lone pair and is trigonal pyramidal. BF₃ is trigonal planar and symmetric, so its three dipoles cancel.

Is SO₂ polar?›

Yes: it is bent. S has a lone pair, so SO₂ is bent and its S–O dipoles add.

Which of these dissolves in water: NH₃ or CCl₄?›

NH₃, because it is polar like water. "Like dissolves like": polar dissolves in polar.

What decides whether a molecule with polar bonds is polar overall?›

Its shape: whether the bond dipoles cancel. Molecular polarity is the vector sum of the bond dipoles.
