Warm-up (5 min)

  1. You push a box with 20 N and it doesn’t move. The most static friction can supply is 80 N. What is the friction force? → 20 N. The box is at rest, so the net force is zero and friction exactly matches the push. 80 N is only the limit.
  2. A ball on a string is whirled in a horizontal circle and the string breaks. Which way does the ball go? → Along the tangent, in a straight line (Newton’s first law). Not straight outward.
  3. The distance between two masses doubles. What happens to the gravitational force? → It becomes ¼ as large, because F∝1/r2F \propto 1/r^2.

Part 1 — Static and Kinetic Friction (15 min)

Two kinds of friction (HMH §4.4, Glencoe §5.2)

Friction comes from the two surfaces catching on each other where they touch. It always acts along the surface and opposes sliding, or the tendency to slide.

  • Static friction fsf_s acts while the surfaces are not sliding. It adjusts to whatever is needed to stop sliding, up to a maximum: fs≤μsNf_s \le \mu_s N.
  • Kinetic friction fkf_k acts while the surfaces are sliding. It is constant: fk=μkNf_k = \mu_k N. It does not depend on the speed or on the area of contact.

μ (mu) is the coefficient of friction. It has no units (μ=f/N\mu = f/N, newtons over newtons), depends only on the two materials, and usually μk<μs\mu_k < \mu_s.

Surfaces (typical values) μ_s μ_k
rubber on dry concrete 0.80 0.65
wood on wood 0.50 0.20
steel on steel (dry) 0.78 0.58
steel on steel (oiled) 0.15 0.06
Teflon on steel 0.04 0.04

The decision rule. Find N. Find fs,max=μsNf_{s,max} = \mu_s N. Compare the applied force along the surface with it:

  • applied force ≤ μsN\mu_s N: it stays at rest, and fsf_s = the applied force;
  • applied force > μsN\mu_s N: it slides, friction becomes fk=μkNf_k = \mu_k N, and a=F−fkma = \dfrac{F - f_k}{m}.

Worked Example 1: the 40 kg crate

A 40 kg crate sits on a level floor with μs=0.50\mu_s = 0.50 and μk=0.35\mu_k = 0.35. (a) A 150 N horizontal push is applied. What is the friction force? (b) What is the smallest push that starts it moving? (c) The push is 250 N. Find the acceleration. (d) You push with exactly the force from (b) and keep pushing with it once the crate moves. Find the acceleration. (e) The crate slides at 4.0 m/s and you let go. How far does it slide, and for how long?

  • N=mg=40×9.81=392.4N = mg = 40 \times 9.81 = 392.4 N. fs,max=0.50×392.4=196.2f_{s,max} = 0.50 \times 392.4 = 196.2 N. fk=0.35×392.4=137.3f_k = 0.35 \times 392.4 = 137.3 N.
  • (a) 150 N < 196.2 N, so it stays at rest. Static friction equals the push: 150 N, opposite the push.
  • (b) The push must just exceed fs,maxf_{s,max}: 196 N.
  • (c) 250 N > 196.2 N, so it slides: a=250−137.340=112.740a = \dfrac{250 - 137.3}{40} = \dfrac{112.7}{40} = 2.82 m/s².
  • (d) Once it moves, friction drops from 196.2 N to 137.3 N, so the same push now accelerates it: a=196.2−137.340a = \dfrac{196.2 - 137.3}{40} = 1.47 m/s². Shortcut: a=(μs−μk)g=0.15×9.81=1.47a = (\mu_s - \mu_k)g = 0.15 \times 9.81 = 1.47 m/s².
  • (e) Only friction acts along the floor: μkmg=ma\mu_k mg = ma, so a=μkg=0.35×9.81=3.43a = \mu_k g = 0.35 \times 9.81 = 3.43 m/s² (slowing). The mass cancels. d=v22a=4.022×3.43d = \dfrac{v^2}{2a} = \dfrac{4.0^2}{2 \times 3.43} = 2.33 m, and t=va=4.03.43t = \dfrac{v}{a} = \dfrac{4.0}{3.43} = 1.17 s.

The normal force is not always mg

N is whatever the surface must push to stop the object sinking into it. Write ∑Fy=0\sum F_y = 0 every time:

  • Pull up at θ: N+Fsinθ−mg=0N + F\sin\theta - mg = 0, so N=mg−FsinθN = mg - F\sin\theta.
  • Push down at θ: N−Fsinθ−mg=0N - F\sin\theta - mg = 0, so N=mg+FsinθN = mg + F\sin\theta.
  • Only the horizontal part, FcosθF\cos\theta, moves the object along the floor.

Worked Example 2: pulling a trunk at an angle

A 45 kg trunk is pulled across a floor by a strap at 35° above the horizontal with a 200 N force. μk=0.30\mu_k = 0.30. Find (a) N, (b) the friction, (c) the acceleration. (d) What if the same 200 N pushed down at 35° instead?

  • mg=45×9.81=441mg = 45 \times 9.81 = 441 N. Fsin35°=200×0.574=115F\sin 35° = 200 \times 0.574 = 115 N. Fcos35°=200×0.819=163.8F\cos 35° = 200 \times 0.819 = 163.8 N.
  • (a) N=441−115N = 441 - 115 = 327 N.
  • (b) fk=0.30×327f_k = 0.30 \times 327 = 98.0 N.
  • (c) a=163.8−98.045a = \dfrac{163.8 - 98.0}{45} = 1.46 m/s². (Using N = mg gives friction 132 N and a = 0.70 m/s²: that is the classic wrong answer.)
  • (d) N=441+115=556N = 441 + 115 = 556 N, so fk=0.30×556=167f_k = 0.30 \times 556 = 167 N, which is more than the 163.8 N forward part. The trunk would slow down: pushing down at this angle cannot keep it moving.

Static friction can push you forward

Static friction opposes slipping, not motion. When you walk, your foot pushes back on the ground, so the ground pushes your foot forward (Newton’s third law), and the foot doesn’t slip, so it is static friction. The same holds for a car’s tires when it speeds up, and for a crate riding on an accelerating truck: friction points forward, in the direction of the acceleration.

Two standard results come from this:

  • Largest acceleration without slipping: μsmg=ma\mu_s mg = ma, so amax=μsga_{max} = \mu_s g. With μs=0.80\mu_s = 0.80: 7.85 m/s².
  • Book held against a wall by a horizontal push F: the push is the normal force (N = F), and friction holds the weight: μsF≥mg\mu_s F \ge mg, so F≥mgμsF \ge \dfrac{mg}{\mu_s}.

Part 2 — Inclined Planes (15 min)

Derivation: resolving the weight (Glencoe §5.3)

Step 1: tilt the axes. The acceleration is along the slope, so put x′ along the slope and y′ perpendicular to it. N and friction already lie on these axes; only mg needs splitting.

Step 2: find the angle. The perpendicular to the slope is tilted from the vertical by the same angle θ that the slope is tilted from the horizontal. So the angle between mg and the y′ axis is θ.

Step 3: resolve. Along the slope (downhill): mgsinθmg\sin\theta. Into the slope: mgcosθmg\cos\theta. Sin to slide, cos to squeeze.

Step 4: the perpendicular axis. The block doesn’t leave or sink into the surface, so ay′=0a_{y'} = 0: N−mgcosθ=0N - mg\cos\theta = 0, which gives N=mgcosθN = mg\cos\theta, less than mg.

Step 5: along the slope. Sliding down with kinetic friction pointing up the slope: mgsinθ−μkmgcosθ=mamg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta = ma, so a=g(sinθ−μkcosθ)a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).

The mass cancels. Limit checks: with no friction, a=gsinθa = g\sin\theta; θ = 0 gives a = 0 (a flat floor); θ = 90° gives a = g (free fall).

Will it slide? A block at rest stays only if the slope component can be held by static friction: mgsinθ≤μsmgcosθmg\sin\theta \le \mu_s mg\cos\theta, that is tanθ≤μs\tan\theta \le \mu_s.

  • Tilt a board until a block just slips: μs=tanθ\mu_s = \tan\theta (the angle of repose, a lab method for μs\mu_s).
  • A block sliding down at constant velocity has balanced forces: μk=tanθ\mu_k = \tan\theta.
  • A block at rest on a slope has fs=mgsinθf_s = mg\sin\theta up the slope, not μsmgcosθ\mu_s mg\cos\theta.

Worked Example 3: a box on a ramp that is raised

A 12 kg box rests on a ramp with μs=0.60\mu_s = 0.60 and μk=0.40\mu_k = 0.40. (a) At 28°, does it slide? If not, find the friction force. (b) At what angle does it start to slip? (c) The ramp is raised to 35°. Find the box’s acceleration.

  • mg=12×9.81=118mg = 12 \times 9.81 = 118 N.
  • (a) mgsin28°=118×0.469=55.3mg\sin 28° = 118 \times 0.469 = 55.3 N. N=mgcos28°=118×0.883=104N = mg\cos 28° = 118 \times 0.883 = 104 N, so fs,max=0.60×104=62.4f_{s,max} = 0.60 \times 104 = 62.4 N. 55.3 N < 62.4 N, so it stays at rest, with friction 55.3 N up the slope. (Check with the shortcut: tan28°=0.53<0.60\tan 28° = 0.53 < 0.60.)
  • (b) θ=tan−1(0.60)\theta = \tan^{-1}(0.60) = 31.0°.
  • (c) tan35°=0.70>0.60\tan 35° = 0.70 > 0.60, so it slides. a=9.81(sin35°−0.40cos35°)=9.81(0.574−0.328)=9.81×0.246a = 9.81(\sin 35° - 0.40\cos 35°) = 9.81(0.574 - 0.328) = 9.81 \times 0.246 = 2.41 m/s² down the slope. (In forces: mgsin35°=67.5mg\sin 35° = 67.5 N, fk=0.40×96.4=38.6f_k = 0.40 \times 96.4 = 38.6 N, a=67.5−38.612=2.41a = \dfrac{67.5 - 38.6}{12} = 2.41 m/s². ✓)

Going up versus coming down

  • Sliding up (after a push): friction points down the slope, with mgsinθmg\sin\theta. Deceleration a=g(sinθ+μkcosθ)a = g(\sin\theta + \mu_k\cos\theta).
  • Sliding down: friction points up the slope. a=g(sinθ−μkcosθ)a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).
  • Pushing up at constant speed: F=mgsinθ+μkmgcosθF = mg\sin\theta + \mu_k mg\cos\theta.

Worked Example 4: launched up a slope

A block is given a push and slides up a 20° incline at 5.0 m/s. μk=0.15\mu_k = 0.15 and μs=0.25\mu_s = 0.25. (a) Find its deceleration on the way up. (b) How far up does it go? (c) Does it slide back? (d) Find its acceleration coming down. (e) How fast is it moving when it gets back to the start?

  • (a) a=9.81(sin20°+0.15cos20°)=9.81(0.342+0.141)=9.81×0.483a = 9.81(\sin 20° + 0.15\cos 20°) = 9.81(0.342 + 0.141) = 9.81 \times 0.483 = 4.74 m/s² (down the slope).
  • (b) d=v22a=5.022×4.74d = \dfrac{v^2}{2a} = \dfrac{5.0^2}{2 \times 4.74} = 2.64 m.
  • (c) tan20°=0.364>μs=0.25\tan 20° = 0.364 > \mu_s = 0.25, so static friction can’t hold it: it slides back down.
  • (d) a=9.81(0.342−0.141)=9.81×0.201a = 9.81(0.342 - 0.141) = 9.81 \times 0.201 = 1.97 m/s².
  • (e) v=2ad=2×1.97×2.64v = \sqrt{2ad} = \sqrt{2 \times 1.97 \times 2.64} = 3.23 m/s. Less than 5.0 m/s, because friction removed energy on both legs.

Part 3 — Universal Gravitation (13 min)

The law (HMH Ch 7, Glencoe §7.1)

Every mass attracts every other mass with a force

F=Gm1m2r2,G=6.67×10−11N·m2/kg2F = \dfrac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}, \qquad G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\ \text{N·m}^2\text{/kg}^2

  • r is measured center to center. A sphere pulls as if all its mass were at its center. For a satellite, r=RE+hr = R_E + h.
  • The force is always attractive and acts across empty space.
  • The two forces are a third-law pair: Earth pulls the Moon exactly as hard as the Moon pulls Earth. The Moon accelerates more because it has less mass.
  • G is tiny, so gravity is noticeable only when one mass is enormous. Cavendish first measured G (1798) with a torsion balance.

The inverse-square law and ratio problems

Most quiz questions on gravitation are ratios. New force = old force × (mass factors) ÷ (distance factor)².

Change Working New force

distance tripled 1/321/3^2 F/9
distance halved 1/(1/2)21/(1/2)^2 4F
one mass doubled 2/12/1 2F

Worked Example 5: you and the Earth

Find the gravitational force between Earth and a 65 kg student standing on its surface, and compare it with mg.

  • F=(6.67×10−11)(5.97×1024)(65)(6.38×106)2=2.588×10164.070×1013F = \dfrac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})(65)}{(6.38 \times 10^6)^2} = \dfrac{2.588 \times 10^{16}}{4.070 \times 10^{13}} = 636 N.
  • mg=65×9.81=638mg = 65 \times 9.81 = 638 N. They agree: your weight is the gravitational force of the Earth on you. (The small difference is because Earth spins and isn’t a perfect sphere. Use 9.81.)

Derivation: where g comes from

The weight of a mass m at distance r from a planet’s center is its gravitational force: mg=GMmr2mg = \dfrac{GMm}{r^2}. The m cancels:

g=GMr2g = \dfrac{GM}{r^2}

So g depends only on the planet’s mass and how far you are from its center: g∝Mr2g \propto \dfrac{M}{r^2}. Its units are N/kg, which equal m/s².

Worked Example 6: g elsewhere

  1. A planet has 3 times Earth’s mass and 1.5 times its radius. Find g on its surface.
  2. Find g 2000 km above Earth’s surface.
  3. At what height above Earth’s surface is g one ninth of its surface value?
  • (a) g=9.81×31.52=9.81×1.33g = 9.81 \times \dfrac{3}{1.5^2} = 9.81 \times 1.33 = 13.1 m/s².
  • (b) r=6.38×106+2.00×106=8.38×106r = 6.38 \times 10^6 + 2.00 \times 10^6 = 8.38 \times 10^6 m. g=(6.67×10−11)(5.97×1024)(8.38×106)2g = \dfrac{(6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24})}{(8.38 \times 10^6)^2} = 5.67 m/s².
  • (c) g∝1/r2g \propto 1/r^2, so one ninth needs r=3REr = 3R_E. The height is h=r−RE=2REh = r - R_E = 2R_E = 1.28 × 10⁷ m (12,800 km). The trap answer is 3R_E: that is the distance from the center, not the height.

Mass versus weight. A 70 kg astronaut has a mass of 70 kg everywhere. Her weight, mg, changes with g: about 113 N on the Moon (g = 1.62 m/s²).

Part 4 — Orbits (7 min)

Derivation: orbital speed and period

For a circular orbit, gravity is the centripetal force:

GMmr2=mv2r⇒v=GMr\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{r}}

  • The satellite’s mass m cancels: a wrench and the ISS in the same orbit move at the same speed.
  • A higher orbit (larger r) is slower.
  • The period is T=2πrvT = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v}. Substituting v and squaring gives Kepler’s third law, T2=4π2GMr3T^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3: higher orbits also take longer.

Worked Example 7: a satellite 1,500 km up

Find the orbital speed and period of a satellite 1,500 km above Earth’s surface.

  • r=6.38×106+1.50×106=7.88×106r = 6.38 \times 10^6 + 1.50 \times 10^6 = 7.88 \times 10^6 m.
  • GME=(6.67×10−11)(5.97×1024)=3.98×1014GM_E = (6.67 \times 10^{-11})(5.97 \times 10^{24}) = 3.98 \times 10^{14} m³/s².
  • v=3.98×10147.88×106v = \sqrt{\dfrac{3.98 \times 10^{14}}{7.88 \times 10^6}} = 7,110 m/s.
  • T=2π(7.88×106)7110=6960T = \dfrac{2\pi(7.88 \times 10^6)}{7110} = 6960 s = 116 min.

Kepler ratio, no G needed: a planet with 9 times the orbital radius has T∝r3/2T \propto r^{3/2}, so its year is 91.59^{1.5} = 27 times as long.

Why astronauts float. At the ISS (400 km up) g is still 8.66 m/s², 88% of the surface value. The astronauts and the station fall around Earth together, so nothing presses on anything: apparent weightlessness, not zero gravity.

Part 5 — Circular Motion (15 min)

Accelerating at constant speed (HMH Ch 7, Glencoe §6.2)

An object moving in a circle at constant speed is accelerating, because the direction of its velocity keeps changing. The acceleration points to the center:

ac=v2r=4π2rT2,v=2πrTa_c = \dfrac{v^2}{r} = \dfrac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}, \qquad v = \dfrac{2\pi r}{T}

By Newton’s second law there must be a net force toward the center, ∑Fin=mv2r\sum F_{in} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}. This centripetal force is not a new kind of force: it is a job, done by a real force.

Situation Who supplies the centripetal force
car on a flat curve static friction from the road
ball whirled on a string tension
Moon, satellites gravity
loop-the-loop, banked road the normal force (with gravity)

If the inward force disappears, the object moves off along the tangent (first law). The “thrown outward” feeling in a turning car is your inertia: there is no outward force on you.

The method: (1) free-body diagram with real forces only; (2) inward is positive; (3) ∑Fin=mv2r\sum F_{in} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}, and ∑F=0\sum F = 0 in any direction with no motion; (4) solve.

Worked Example 8: a car on a flat curve

A 1500 kg car takes a flat curve of radius 60 m on a wet road with μs=0.55\mu_s = 0.55. (a) Find the fastest safe speed. (b) Find the friction force at 15 m/s. (c) What happens at 30 m/s?

  • Vertically N=mgN = mg. Friction is the only horizontal force, so it supplies mv2r\dfrac{mv^2}{r}, and it can be at most μsmg\mu_s mg.
  • (a) μsmg=mv2r\mu_s mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}, so vmax=μsgr=0.55×9.81×60=324v_{max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r} = \sqrt{0.55 \times 9.81 \times 60} = \sqrt{324} = 18.0 m/s. The mass cancels.
  • (b) f=1500×15260f = \dfrac{1500 \times 15^2}{60} = 5,630 N (the limit is 0.55×1500×9.81=8,0900.55 \times 1500 \times 9.81 = 8{,}090 N, so it holds).
  • (c) Doubling v multiplies mv2r\dfrac{mv^2}{r} by 4: 22,500 N, far more than 8,090 N. The car skids, moving off along the tangent toward the outside of the curve.

Worked Example 9: a ball on a string in a vertical circle

A 0.60 kg ball swings in a vertical circle on a 1.1 m string. (a) What is the slowest speed at the top that keeps the string tight? (b) Find the tension at the top at 4.5 m/s. (c) Find the tension at the bottom at 7.0 m/s.

  • mg=0.60×9.81=5.89mg = 0.60 \times 9.81 = 5.89 N.
  • (a) At the slowest speed T = 0 and gravity alone gives the centripetal force: mg=mv2rmg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}, v=gr=9.81×1.1v = \sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{9.81 \times 1.1} = 3.28 m/s.
  • (b) Top: both forces point down, toward the center: T+mg=mv2rT + mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}. T=0.60×4.521.1−5.89=11.0−5.89T = \dfrac{0.60 \times 4.5^2}{1.1} - 5.89 = 11.0 - 5.89 = 5.16 N.
  • (c) Bottom: T points up (inward), mg down (outward): T−mg=mv2rT - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}. T=0.60×7.021.1+5.89=26.7+5.89T = \dfrac{0.60 \times 7.0^2}{1.1} + 5.89 = 26.7 + 5.89 = 32.6 N. The string is most likely to break here.

Worked Example 10: a hill and a dip

A 1200 kg car moves at 14 m/s over a hilltop and through a dip, each with radius 35 m. Find the normal force at each, and the speed at which it would leave the road at the top.

  • mg=1200×9.81=11,770mg = 1200 \times 9.81 = 11{,}770 N. mv2r=1200×14235=6,720\dfrac{mv^2}{r} = \dfrac{1200 \times 14^2}{35} = 6{,}720 N.
  • Hilltop (center below, down is inward): mg−N=mv2rmg - N = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}, so N=11,770−6,720N = 11{,}770 - 6{,}720 = 5,050 N. Passengers feel lighter.
  • Dip (center above, up is inward): N−mg=mv2rN - mg = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}, so N=11,770+6,720N = 11{,}770 + 6{,}720 = 18,500 N. Passengers feel heavier.

The banked curve

With no friction, only N and mg act. Vertically Ncosθ=mgN\cos\theta = mg; toward the center Nsinθ=mv2rN\sin\theta = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}. Dividing the second by the first:

tanθ=v2rg\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^2}{rg}

Example: a curve of radius 90 m is designed for 20 m/s. tanθ=20290×9.81=0.453\tan\theta = \dfrac{20^2}{90 \times 9.81} = 0.453, so θ = 24.4°.

  • Faster than the design speed: the car tends to slide up and out, so friction acts down the slope.
  • Slower: it tends to slide down and in, so friction acts up the slope.

The elevator (apparent weight)

A scale reads the normal force. Take up as positive: N−mg=maN - mg = ma.

“Accelerating upward” includes moving up and speeding up and moving down and slowing down.

Third-law pairs

A pair always involves two objects that swap places (“A pushes B” and “B pushes A”), the forces are equal and opposite, and they are the same type of force. They act on different objects, so they never cancel.

  • The weight of a book and the table’s normal force on it are not a pair (same object, different types). The pair to “Earth pulls the book down” is “the book pulls Earth up”.
  • A truck and a small car collide: the forces on them are equal. The car has less mass, so its acceleration (and damage) is larger.
  • A horse and cart pull each other equally. They speed up because the ground pushes the horse forward harder than the cart pulls back.

Connected objects

Treat the whole system to find the acceleration (internal tensions cancel), then one object to find the tension.

  • Atwood machine, 4.0 kg and 6.0 kg over a pulley: a=(m2−m1)gm1+m2=2.0×9.8110a = \dfrac{(m_2 - m_1)g}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{2.0 \times 9.81}{10} = 1.96 m/s². For the 4.0 kg mass: T−m1g=m1aT - m_1 g = m_1 a, T=4.0(9.81+1.96)T = 4.0(9.81 + 1.96) = 47.1 N.

Most common mistakes

  1. Writing f=μsNf = \mu_s N for a box that isn’t moving. Static friction equals the push; μsN\mu_s N is only the limit.
  2. Using N = mg with an angled force or on an incline. Always get N from the perpendicular direction first.
  3. Keeping μs\mu_s after the object starts sliding. Moving means μk\mu_k.
  4. Drawing mgsinθmg\sin\theta as an extra force on top of mg, or drawing a forward “force of motion”.
  5. Getting friction’s direction wrong on an incline. It opposes the sliding, so it flips when the block goes up.
  6. Forgetting to square r, or using the height instead of the distance from the center.
  7. Drawing a centripetal or “centrifugal” arrow. The centripetal force is the net inward force made of the real forces.
  8. Signs in a vertical circle. At the top, tension and weight both point inward (down).
  9. Saying there is no gravity in orbit. Astronauts float because they are in free fall.
