Part 1 — Work (12 min)

What “work” means in physics (NCERT Work and Energy, Glencoe §10.1, HMH Ch 5)

In everyday language, holding a heavy bag for ten minutes is hard work. In physics it is no work at all on the bag, because the bag doesn’t move. Physics work needs two things: a force, and a displacement with part of it along the force.

Definition (work by a constant force). The work W done on an object by a constant force F is the component of the force along the displacement multiplied by the size of the displacement:

W=(Fcosθ)d=FdcosθW = (F\cos\theta)\,d = Fd\cos\theta
where θ is the angle between the force and the displacement. Work is a scalar, measured in joules: 1 J is the work done by a 1 N force acting through 1 m.

Where cosθ\cos\theta comes from

Split the force into two perpendicular components, exactly as in Session 3:

  • along the displacement: FcosθF\cos\theta. This part pushes the box the way it moves.
  • perpendicular to it: FsinθF\sin\theta. The box moves zero distance in this direction, so this part does nothing to move it.

Only the first part counts, so W=(Fcosθ)×dW = (F\cos\theta) \times d. When the force points exactly along the motion, θ = 0 and W=FdW = Fd, the simple “force × distance” from Class 9.

The sign of work

Angle θ cosθ\cos\theta Work Effect on the object Examples
0° +1 +Fd+Fd speeds it up pushing a cart forward
0° to 90° positive positive speeds it up pulling a sled with a rope at 30°
90° 0 zero no change in speed normal force on a level road; gravity on a ball rolling on a level floor; the string’s tension on a ball moving in a circle
90° to 180° negative negative slows it down friction on a sliding box; brakes
180° −1 −Fd-Fd slows it down friction directly opposite the motion

Link to Session 4. In uniform circular motion the net force points to the center and the velocity is along the tangent, so θ = 90° at every instant. The force does no work, which is exactly why the speed stays constant while the direction keeps changing.

Net work

When several forces act, find the work done by each force with W1, then add them (work is a scalar, so ordinary addition, with signs):

Wnet=W1+W2+W3+…W_{net} = W_1 + W_2 + W_3 + \dots

For constant forces along a straight path, this equals FnetdF_{net}\,d. That gives a useful check.

Worked Example 1: the sled from D4

The sled from D4 moves 5.0 m. Find the work done by each force and the net work.

  • Pull: WF=Fdcos30°=50×5.0×0.866=+217W_F = Fd\cos30° = 50 \times 5.0 \times 0.866 = +217 J.
  • Friction: it points backward, θ = 180°: Wf=7.31×5.0×(−1)=−36.6W_f = 7.31 \times 5.0 \times (-1) = -36.6 J.
  • Gravity and normal force: both vertical, θ = 90°: Wg=WN=0W_g = W_N = 0.
  • Net work: Wnet=216.5−36.6+0+0=+180W_{net} = 216.5 - 36.6 + 0 + 0 = +180 J.
  • Check with W2: Fnet=43.3−7.31=36.0F_{net} = 43.3 - 7.31 = 36.0 N, and Fnetd=36.0×5.0=180F_{net}\,d = 36.0 \times 5.0 = 180 J ✓

Worked Example 2: lifting at constant velocity

You lift a 12 kg box 1.5 m straight up at constant velocity. Find the work done by you, by gravity, and the net work.

  • Constant velocity, so your force equals the weight: F=mg=12×9.81=117.7F = mg = 12 \times 9.81 = 117.7 N, pointing up, along the motion.
  • You: Wyou=117.7×1.5=+177W_{you} = 117.7 \times 1.5 = +177 J.
  • Gravity: it points down while the box moves up, θ = 180°: Wg=−177W_g = -177 J.
  • Net work: 177−177=0177 - 177 = 0. The speed doesn’t change, so the net work is zero. Part 2 shows this is not a coincidence.

Work is the area under a force–displacement graph

For a constant force, the graph of F against d is a horizontal line, and W=FdW = Fd is the area of the rectangle under it. The same idea works when the force changes: the work is still the area under the graph.

The spring. Hooke’s law says the force needed to stretch a spring by x is F=kxF = kx, where k is the spring constant. The graph is a straight line through the origin, so the area is a triangle:

W=12×base×height=12(x)(kx)=12kx2W = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \text{base} \times \text{height} = \tfrac{1}{2}(x)(kx) = \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2

For k = 200 N/m, stretching from 0 to 0.15 m takes 12(200)(0.15)2=2.25\tfrac{1}{2}(200)(0.15)^2 = 2.25 J, but the next 0.15 m takes 6.75 J, three times as much, because the spring pulls back harder the more it is stretched. This 12kx2\tfrac{1}{2}kx^2 returns in Session 9 as elastic potential energy.

Part 2 — Kinetic Energy and the Work–Energy Theorem (13 min)

Derivation (Glencoe §11.1, HMH Ch 5)

Take an object of mass m. A constant net force FnetF_{net} acts along its motion, and its speed changes from u to v over a displacement d.

Step 1: net work. Wnet=FnetdW_{net} = F_{net}\,d.

Step 2: Newton’s second law. Fnet=maF_{net} = ma, so Wnet=madW_{net} = m\,a\,d.

Step 3: kinematics (Session 1). v2=u2+2adv^2 = u^2 + 2ad, so ad=v2−u22a\,d = \dfrac{v^2 - u^2}{2}.

Step 4: substitute.

Wnet=m⋅v2−u22=12mv2−12mu2W_{net} = m \cdot \frac{v^2 - u^2}{2} = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}mu^2

The quantity 12mv2\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 appears on both sides of the change, before and after. It is called the kinetic energy, the energy an object has because it is moving:

KE=12mv2KE = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2

Work–energy theorem. The net work done on an object equals the change in its kinetic energy:

Wnet=ΔKE=KEf−KEiW_{net} = \Delta KE = KE_f - KE_i

What the theorem says in words:

  • Positive net work speeds an object up; negative net work slows it down; zero net work leaves its speed unchanged (Worked Example 2, and circular motion).
  • KE depends on v2v^2: doubling the speed quadruples the kinetic energy, and so quadruples the work needed to stop it.
  • KE is a scalar and never negative. Direction doesn’t matter: a 2 kg ball at 3 m/s has 9 J whether it moves up, down or sideways.
  • Energy is measured in the same unit as work, joules, because work is how energy is transferred.

Worked Example 3: the sled’s speed

The sled from Worked Example 1 was already moving at 1.0 m/s when the 5.0 m pull began. Find its final speed.

  • Wnet=+180W_{net} = +180 J (Example 1) and KEi=12(10)(1.0)2=5.0KE_i = \tfrac{1}{2}(10)(1.0)^2 = 5.0 J.
  • KEf=KEi+Wnet=5.0+180=185KE_f = KE_i + W_{net} = 5.0 + 180 = 185 J.
  • 12(10)v2=185\tfrac{1}{2}(10)v^2 = 185, so v2=37.0v^2 = 37.0 and v=6.08v = 6.08 m/s.
  • Check with kinematics: a=3.60a = 3.60 m/s² (D4), so v2=1.02+2(3.60)(5.0)=37.0v^2 = 1.0^2 + 2(3.60)(5.0) = 37.0 ✓. Same answer, but the energy method never needed a.

Worked Example 4: stopping distance

A 1200 kg car skids to a stop with its wheels locked on a road with μk=0.70\mu_k = 0.70. Find the skidding distance from (a) 15 m/s, (b) 30 m/s.

  • Only friction does work horizontally: f=μkmgf = \mu_k mg, acting backward, so Wnet=−μkmgdW_{net} = -\mu_k mg\,d.
  • Final KE is zero: −μkmgd=0−12mv2-\mu_k mg\,d = 0 - \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2.
  • The mass cancels: d=v22μkgd = \dfrac{v^2}{2\mu_k g} (formula SD).
  • (a) d=1522(0.70)(9.81)=22513.73=16.4d = \dfrac{15^2}{2(0.70)(9.81)} = \dfrac{225}{13.73} = 16.4 m.
  • (b) d=30213.73=65.5d = \dfrac{30^2}{13.73} = 65.5 m.

Doubling the speed makes the skid four times as long. This is why speed limits drop near schools.

Worked Example 5: the incline from Session 6, by energy

A 5.0 kg block slides 2.0 m from rest down a 30° incline with μk=0.20\mu_k = 0.20 (Session 6, Worked Example 4). Find its speed at the bottom.

  • Gravity’s component along the slope does the positive work: Wg=(mgsin30°)d=24.5×2.0=+49.1W_g = (mg\sin30°)\,d = 24.5 \times 2.0 = +49.1 J.
  • Friction: f=μkmgcos30°=0.20×42.5=8.50f = \mu_k mg\cos30° = 0.20 \times 42.5 = 8.50 N, opposite the motion: Wf=−8.50×2.0=−17.0W_f = -8.50 \times 2.0 = -17.0 J.
  • The normal force is perpendicular to the slope: WN=0W_N = 0.
  • Wnet=49.1−17.0=32.1W_{net} = 49.1 - 17.0 = 32.1 J =12(5.0)v2= \tfrac{1}{2}(5.0)v^2, so v2=12.8v^2 = 12.8 and v=3.58v = 3.58 m/s.
  • Check: Session 6 found a = 3.21 m/s², and v2=2(3.21)(2.0)=12.8v^2 = 2(3.21)(2.0) = 12.8 ✓

Part 3 — Power (5 min)

Definition (NCERT Work and Energy, Glencoe §10.1, HMH Ch 5)

Two students of the same mass climb the same stairs. They do the same work against gravity, but the one who runs up is more powerful.

Power is the rate at which work is done (the rate at which energy is transferred):

P=WtP = \frac{W}{t}
Unit: the watt, 1 W = 1 J/s. Large units: 1 kW = 1000 W; 1 horsepower = 746 W.

Derivation: P=FvP = Fv

If a constant force F acts along the motion of an object moving at constant speed v:

P=Wt=Fdt=F⋅dt=FvP = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{Fd}{t} = F\cdot\frac{d}{t} = Fv

This is how engines are rated: at a steady speed, the engine’s force balances the drag, and the power needed is drag × speed.

Worked Example 6

(a) A 55 kg student climbs stairs that rise 3.0 m in 4.0 s. Find her power.

  • Work against gravity: W=mgh=55×9.81×3.0=1620W = mgh = 55 \times 9.81 \times 3.0 = 1620 J.
  • P=W/t=1620/4.0=405P = W/t = 1620/4.0 = 405 W, about 0.54 hp.

(b) An elevator motor lifts an 800 kg car at a constant 2.0 m/s. Find the power output.

  • Constant velocity, so the cable force is F=mg=800×9.81=7850F = mg = 800 \times 9.81 = 7850 N.
  • P=Fv=7850×2.0=15700P = Fv = 7850 \times 2.0 = 15\,700 W =15.7= 15.7 kW.

Most common mistakes

  1. Using the whole force instead of the component along the motion. For an angled pull, W=FdcosθW = Fd\cos\theta, not Fd.
  2. Forgetting that friction does negative work, or giving friction’s work a positive sign and then adding it.
  3. Using N = mg when a rope pulls at an angle. The rope’s vertical component changes N, and so friction (D4, Q5). This was a midterm-type error.
  4. Squaring the change in speed instead of the speeds: ΔKE=12m(v2−u2)\Delta KE = \tfrac{1}{2}m(v^2 - u^2), never 12m(v−u)2\tfrac{1}{2}m(v - u)^2 (Exit ticket Q1).
  5. Mixing up work by one force and net work. Only the net work equals ΔKE.
  6. Forgetting unit conversions: grams to kilograms, centimeters to meters, kW to W.
  7. Saying “holding something up is work.” No displacement, no work done on the object.
