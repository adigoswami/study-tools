## The Framework — Electronegativity & Bond Character (7 min)

Recap definition (from the trends session): **electronegativity** is the ability of an atom in a compound to attract shared electrons to itself (Pauling scale, F = 4.0 highest).

> **The core idea (NCERT Class 11, section 4.3.6):** no real bond is 100% ionic or 100% covalent — it's a continuum, and the electronegativity *difference* (ΔEN) between the two bonded atoms tells you where a bond sits on it.

- **ΔEN = 0 → nonpolar covalent bond.** When two *identical* atoms bond (H₂, O₂, Cl₂, N₂, F₂), the shared electron pair sits exactly between the two nuclei — neither atom pulls harder.
- **Small-to-moderate ΔEN → polar covalent bond.** Textbook's own example: H–F. Fluorine (EN 4.0) pulls the shared pair toward itself more than hydrogen (EN 2.1) does. The pair is still *shared*, just unevenly — this gives the molecule a **dipole moment** (a partial positive end, δ+, and partial negative end, δ−).
- **Large ΔEN → ionic bond.** The pull is so lopsided the electron transfers completely rather than staying shared — this is exactly the Na/Cl picture from the ionic-bonding topic.

**Working rule of thumb for placing a bond on the spectrum** (a general-chemistry convention, not a number NCERT states — say this explicitly, since the textbook describes the change qualitatively, not with a cutoff): ΔEN < 0.5 → nonpolar covalent · 0.5–1.7 → polar covalent · > 1.7 → mostly ionic.

**Worked example:** H–F: ΔEN = |2.1 − 4.0| = 1.9 → past the 1.7 line, yet H and F are both non-metals sharing electrons — so it's *strongly polar covalent*, not truly ionic. This is exactly why the line is a guide, not a wall: bond character changes continuously, and real compounds don't sort neatly into three boxes.

This is the lens for the rest of today — every bond you look at next, ask "what's the ΔEN telling me here?"

## Metallic Bonds, Properties of Pure Metals, Alloys (5 min)

**Interesting wrinkle worth raising:** ΔEN between two identical metal atoms is also 0 — same as H₂. But two metal atoms don't behave like H₂. Why not? Because metallic bonding isn't a *localized* shared pair between two atoms at all.

> **Metallic bond (standard chemistry model — not covered in either NCERT chapter, flagged here as before):** metal atoms release their valence electrons into a shared, mobile "sea" of delocalized electrons; what remains is a lattice of positive metal ions held together by electrostatic attraction to that electron sea.

**Properties of pure metals, explained by the model** (NCERT Class 10 Ch3):

- **Malleable** (hammers into sheets) and **ductile** (draws into wires) — ion layers slide past each other while the electron sea flows around them; the bond doesn't break.
- **Good conductors of heat and electricity** — the delocalized electrons carry charge and energy freely.
- **Lustrous** — free electrons reflect light.
- **Sonorous** — pure metals produce a ringing sound when struck (this is why school bells are cast from metal alloys, not non-metals).
- **Generally high melting points** — strong electrostatic attraction between ions and electron sea.

**Alloys (brief — full depth was already covered last session):** a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal, made deliberately to improve properties (strength, hardness, corrosion resistance). Quick recall set: brass (Cu+Zn), bronze (Cu+Sn), stainless steel (Fe+Ni+Cr), solder (Pb+Sn). Alloys generally conduct less well and melt at a lower temperature than the pure metal.

## Ionic Bonds, Ionic Ratios & Polyatomic Ions (9 min)

**Recap via the framework:** an ionic bond sits at the large-ΔEN end of the spectrum — electron transfer, not sharing. Na (EN 0.9) + Cl (EN 3.0): ΔEN = 2.1, well past the ionic line.

**Ionic ratio of compounds — the criss-cross method** (recap from the test):

1. Write the cation symbol first, then the anion symbol, with charges underneath.
2. Criss-cross the charge *numbers* into subscripts.
3. Simplify subscripts by their common factor, if any (e.g. Mg²⁺ + O²⁻ → Mg₂O₂ → **MgO**).

**Polyatomic ions — ratios and writing:** same criss-cross method, treating the whole polyatomic ion as one unit. Use **brackets** whenever 2+ of the *same* polyatomic ion appear:

| Compound | Cation | Anion | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium carbonate | Ca²⁺ | CO₃²⁻ | CaCO₃ *(2:2 simplifies to 1:1, no brackets needed)* |
| Magnesium hydroxide | Mg²⁺ | OH⁻ | Mg(OH)₂ *(2 hydroxide ions — brackets required)* |
| Aluminium sulfate | Al³⁺ | SO₄²⁻ | Al₂(SO₄)₃ *(3 sulfate ions — brackets required)* |
| Ammonium sulfate | NH₄⁺ | SO₄²⁻ | (NH₄)₂SO₄ *(2 ammonium ions — brackets on the cation this time)* |

Quick recall table (already solid from the test — trigger, don't re-teach):

| Polyatomic ions | | Tricky monoatomic ions | |
|---|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ hydroxide, NO₃⁻ nitrate | CO₃²⁻ carbonate, SO₄²⁻ sulfate | Fe²⁺ ferrous / Fe³⁺ ferric | Cu⁺ cuprous / Cu²⁺ cupric |

## Covalent Bonds + Naming with Prefixes (10 min)

**Definition:** a covalent bond forms when two atoms *share* one or more electron pairs, rather than transferring them — the low-ΔEN end of the spectrum. Already touched on via Ch9 9.4.1 (grade-9 foundation); today's focus is *naming* covalent compounds correctly.

**The Greek prefix system** (NCERT Class 9, Ch9, section 9.4.2):

| 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| mono- | di- | tri- | tetra- | penta- | hexa- | hepta- |

**The rules, with verified textbook examples:**

1. **First element** is named plainly — *mono-* is dropped for the first element (CO₂ = carbon dioxide, not "monocarbon dioxide"). It only gets a prefix when there's more than one atom of it (N₂O₄ = **di**nitrogen tetroxide).
2. **Second element** always takes a prefix (mono- included) plus the *-ide* ending (CO = carbon **mon**oxide).
3. **Dropped vowel:** the final *a* or *o* of a prefix is dropped before "oxide" — mon**ox**ide, tetr**ox**ide, pent**ox**ide (never "monooxide," "tetraoxide," "pentaoxide"). N₂O₅ = dinitrogen **pent**oxide.
4. **Hydrogen-first exception:** when hydrogen is the first element, it never takes a prefix, whatever the count — H₂S = hydrogen sulfide, *not* "dihydrogen sulfide."
5. **Common-name exception:** a few compounds are known only by their common names, not the systematic ones — H₂O is "water" (not "hydrogen monoxide"), NH₃ is "ammonia" (its systematic name would be nitrogen trihydride).

**Worked set to run through together:** CO → carbon monoxide · CS₂ → carbon disulfide · PCl₃ → phosphorus trichloride · SF₆ → sulfur hexafluoride · N₂O₄ → dinitrogen tetroxide.

**Making the formula from the name (reverse direction):** read the prefixes directly as subscripts. "Phosphorus trichloride" → P gets no subscript (mono- implied, dropped), Cl gets 3 → PCl₃. "Sulfur hexafluoride" → SF₆.
