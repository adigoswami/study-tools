## 1. Metallic Bonds and Alloys

### Metallic bonding (not in your two chapters — standard chemistry model, included so this topic is covered)

Metal atoms in a solid release their loosely-held outer (valence) electrons into a shared "sea" that moves freely around the whole structure. What's left behind is a lattice of positively charged metal ions sitting inside this sea of mobile ("delocalized") electrons.

**Metallic bond = the electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the surrounding sea of delocalized electrons.**

This one model explains all the typical metal properties:

| Property | Why (from the electron-sea model) |
|---|---|
| Good conductor of electricity & heat | Free electrons carry charge/energy easily |
| Malleable (hammers into sheets) | Layers of ions slide past each other; electron sea just flows around them, bond doesn't break |
| Ductile (draws into wires) | Same reason as malleability |
| Lustrous (shiny) | Free electrons reflect light |
| Generally high melting/boiling point | Strong attraction between ions and electron sea |

### Alloys (this part **is** in your Ch5 textbook)

- Metals do **not** dissolve into each other at room temperature.
- Melt two or more metals at high temperature → they mix into a solution → cool it → it solidifies into a new material that looks like a single metal.
- **Definition:** an alloy is a **homogeneous mixture** of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal.
- Alloys **cannot** be separated by physical methods.
- **Why make alloys?** To get materials that are stronger, more rigid, or more corrosion-resistant than the pure metal.
- An alloy is a **mixture, not a compound** — no new chemical bond forms between the different metal atoms; it's physical mixing at the atomic scale.

**Memorize these three (straight from the textbook):**

| Alloy | Composition |
|---|---|
| Brass | ~80% copper + ~20% zinc |
| Bronze | ~80% copper + ~20% tin |
| Stainless steel | Iron + carbon (0.03–0.8%) + chromium (16–18%) + nickel (10–14%) + molybdenum (2–3%) |

## 2. Ionic Bonding

**Rule of thumb:** fewer than 4 valence electrons → atom **donates/loses** electrons → forms a **positive** ion. More than 4 valence electrons → atom usually **gains or shares** electrons to complete its octet.

> **Ionic bond** = the electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions that holds them together.

Formed by **transfer of electrons**, typically from a metal atom to a non-metal atom.

### Classic example: sodium chloride (NaCl)

- Sodium (Na): 1 valence electron → **loses it** → becomes **Na<sup>+</sup>** (sodium cation) — 11 protons, 10 electrons.
- Chlorine (Cl): 7 valence electrons → **gains 1 electron** → becomes **Cl<sup>–</sup>** (chloride anion).
- Na<sup>+</sup> and Cl<sup>–</sup> attract each other electrostatically → ionic bond → NaCl.

**Vocabulary:**

- **Cation** = positively charged ion (atom **lost** electrons).
- **Anion** = negatively charged ion (atom **gained** electrons).
- Cations + anions, together = "ions."

**Ionic compounds form 3-D crystal lattices, not molecules.** In NaCl, each Na<sup>+</sup> is surrounded by 6 Cl<sup>–</sup>, and each Cl<sup>–</sup> is surrounded by 6 Na<sup>+</sup>, in a repeating pattern (a crystal lattice).

### Naming ionic compounds

- Cation name first, then anion name.
- Simple anion names end in **"-ide"** (chloride, oxide, sulfide...).
- Metals form cations; non-metals form anions.
- Polyatomic ion names generally do **not** end in "-ide" (see Section 3).

### Writing formulas — the criss-cross method

1. Write the cation symbol first, then the anion symbol.
2. Write the charges under the symbols (not as superscripts).
3. Criss-cross the numbers (only) to get the subscripts.
4. Simplify the subscripts by their common factor, if any.

**Worked examples:**

| Compound | Cation | Anion | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calcium chloride | Ca<sup>2+</sup> | Cl<sup>–</sup> | CaCl₂ |
| Aluminium oxide | Al<sup>3+</sup> | O<sup>2–</sup> | Al₂O₃ |
| Magnesium oxide | Mg<sup>2+</sup> | O<sup>2–</sup> | MgO *(2:2 simplifies to 1:1)* |
| Calcium carbonate | Ca<sup>2+</sup> | CO₃<sup>2–</sup> | CaCO₃ *(2:2 simplifies to 1:1)* |
| Magnesium hydroxide | Mg<sup>2+</sup> | OH<sup>–</sup> | Mg(OH)₂ |
| Aluminium hydroxide | Al<sup>3+</sup> | OH<sup>–</sup> | Al(OH)₃ |
| Aluminium sulfate | Al<sup>3+</sup> | SO₄<sup>2–</sup> | Al₂(SO₄)₃ |

**Brackets ( )** are only needed when **2 or more of the same polyatomic ion** appear in the formula.

### Properties of ionic compounds

| Property | Ionic compounds |
|---|---|
| Solubility | Soluble in water; insoluble in kerosene/petrol |
| Conducts electricity as a solid? | **No** — ions are locked in the lattice, can't move |
| Conducts electricity molten or dissolved? | **Yes** — ions become free to move |
| Melting / boiling point | High — strong inter-ionic attraction |

## 3. Polyatomic Ions

**Definition:** ions made of **two or more atoms bonded together**, carrying one overall charge. Names generally do **not** end in "-ide."

| Name of ion | Formula | Valency |
|---|---|---|
| Hydroxide | OH<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Nitrate | NO₃<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate) | HCO₃<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Carbonate | CO₃<sup>2–</sup> | 2 |
| Sulfate | SO₄<sup>2–</sup> | 2 |
| Ammonium | NH₄<sup>+</sup> | 1 |

**Watch out:** Ammonium (NH₄<sup>+</sup>) is the only **cation** in this table — every other polyatomic ion here is an anion.

## 4. Monoatomic Ions

**Definition:** an ion made from a **single atom** that has gained or lost electrons (contrast with polyatomic ions, made of multiple bonded atoms).

**Cations (metals — lose electrons):**

| Name of ion | Formula | Valency |
|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Na<sup>+</sup> | 1 |
| Lithium | Li<sup>+</sup> | 1 |
| Potassium | K<sup>+</sup> | 1 |
| Silver | Ag<sup>+</sup> | 1 |
| Calcium | Ca<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Barium | Ba<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Iron (Ferrous) | Fe<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Iron (Ferric) | Fe<sup>3+</sup> | 3 |
| Copper (Cuprous) | Cu<sup>+</sup> | 1 |
| Copper (Cupric) | Cu<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Magnesium | Mg<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Zinc | Zn<sup>2+</sup> | 2 |
| Aluminium | Al<sup>3+</sup> | 3 |

**Anions (non-metals — gain electrons):**

| Name of ion | Formula | Valency |
|---|---|---|
| Fluoride | F<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Chloride | Cl<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Bromide | Br<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Iodide | I<sup>–</sup> | 1 |
| Oxide | O<sup>2–</sup> | 2 |
| Sulfide | S<sup>2–</sup> | 2 |

**Trickiest pair to double-check:** iron and copper each have **two** possible ions — know which name goes with which charge:

- Ferrous = Fe<sup>2+</sup> · Ferric = Fe<sup>3+</sup>
- Cuprous = Cu<sup>+</sup> · Cupric = Cu<sup>2+</sup>
