Electronegativity & Bond Character 18 cards
What is electronegativity?›

The ability of an atom in a chemical compound to attract the shared electron pair towards itself.

Can electronegativity be measured directly, like ionization enthalpy?›

No — it isn't a measurable quantity. It's a relative number on a scale.

Which electronegativity scale is most widely used, and what value does fluorine get?›

The Pauling scale. Fluorine = 4.0, the most electronegative element.

How does electronegativity change across a period (left → right)?›

It increases, e.g. Li 1.0 → F 4.0. Atoms get smaller, so the nucleus pulls harder on the bonding electrons.

How does electronegativity change down a group?›

It decreases, e.g. F 4.0 → Cl 3.0 → Br 2.8 → I 2.5. Atoms get bigger, so the bonding electrons are farther from the nucleus.

How is electronegativity linked to metallic / non-metallic character?›

High EN means more non-metallic. Low EN means more metallic. So non-metallic character increases across a period, and metallic character increases down a group.

What is ΔEN, and how do you calculate it?›

The difference in electronegativity between the two bonded atoms: ΔEN = |EN(A) − EN(B)|. The bigger ΔEN is, the more ionic the bond.

Is any bond 100% ionic or 100% covalent?›

No. Real bonds have partial ionic and partial covalent character. Bond type is a spectrum.

ΔEN = 0 gives which bond type? Give examples.›

Nonpolar covalent: the pair is shared equally. Examples: H₂, O₂, N₂, F₂, Cl₂ (two identical atoms).

A small-to-moderate ΔEN gives which bond type?›

Polar covalent: the pair is still shared, but pulled toward the more electronegative atom. Example: H–F.

A large ΔEN gives which bond type?›

Ionic: the electron is effectively transferred. Example: NaCl.

What are the rule-of-thumb ΔEN cutoffs?›

Below ~0.5 → nonpolar covalent · ~0.5–1.7 → polar covalent · above ~1.7 → ionic. beyond NCERT

In H–F, which atom is δ+ and which is δ−?›

F is δ− (EN 4.0, pulls the pair toward itself). H is δ+ (EN 2.1).

What does δ (delta) mean in δ+ and δ−?›

A partial charge, less than a full +1 or −1, because the electrons are only shifted, not fully transferred.

What is a dipole moment?›

A measure of how polar a bond is: μ = charge × distance between the charge centres, in debye (D). Polar bonds have one; nonpolar bonds don't.

ΔEN for Na–Cl (0.9, 3.0)? Bond type?›

|0.9 − 3.0| = 2.1 → ionic.

ΔEN for H–Cl (2.1, 3.0)? Bond type?›

|2.1 − 3.0| = 0.9 → polar covalent, with Cl as δ−.

ΔEN for Mg–O (1.2, 3.5)? Bond type?›

|1.2 − 3.5| = 2.3 → ionic.

Metallic Bonds & Pure Metals 15 cards
What is a metallic bond?›

The electrostatic attraction between positive metal ions and the surrounding sea of delocalized electrons. beyond NCERT

What two parts make up the electron-sea model of a metal?›

A lattice of positive metal ions + a sea of delocalized (mobile) electrons. beyond NCERT

Why are metals good conductors of electricity and heat?›

The delocalized electrons are free to move, carrying charge and energy easily. beyond NCERT

Why are metals malleable (can be hammered into sheets)?›

Layers of metal ions can slide past each other while the electron sea flows around them, so the bond doesn't break. beyond NCERT

Why are metals ductile (can be drawn into wires)?›

Same reason as malleability — ion layers slide past each other without breaking the metallic bond. beyond NCERT

Why are metals lustrous (shiny)?›

The free electrons in the electron sea reflect light. beyond NCERT

List the physical properties of pure metals.›

Lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity, sonorous, generally hard, with high melting points.

What does sonorous mean?›

Makes a ringing sound when struck (which is why bells are made of metal).

Which metals are the best conductors of heat? Which are poor ones?›

Best: silver and copper. Poor: lead and mercury.

Name a metal that is liquid at room temperature.›

Mercury.

Name metals soft enough to cut with a knife, and metals that melt in your palm.›

Cut with a knife: sodium, potassium (and lithium). Melt in your palm: gallium and caesium.

Why do most metals have high melting points?›

The attraction between the metal ions and the electron sea is strong and runs through the whole lattice, so breaking it takes a lot of energy. beyond NCERT

Metallic bond vs ionic bond: what's the key difference?›

Metallic: positive metal ions in a sea of shared, delocalized electrons (metal + metal). Ionic: electrons are transferred from a metal to a non-metal, and the oppositely charged ions attract in a lattice.

Why can a metal be hammered flat, while an ionic crystal shatters?›

In a metal, layers slide and the electron sea keeps holding them together. In an ionic crystal, shifting a layer brings like charges face to face. They repel, so the crystal cracks. beyond NCERT

Why do solid metals conduct electricity when solid ionic compounds don't?›

Metals have free-moving electrons. In an ionic solid the ions are locked in place, so there are no mobile charges until it's melted or dissolved.

Alloys 14 cards
Define an alloy.›

A homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a non-metal.

Can metals dissolve into each other at room temperature?›

No — only when melted at high temperature. The hot mixture solidifies into an alloy on cooling.

Can the components of an alloy be separated by physical methods?›

No.

Why are alloys made instead of using pure metals?›

To get materials that are stronger, more rigid, or more corrosion-resistant than the pure metal.

What is the composition of brass?›

~80% copper + ~20% zinc.

What is the composition of bronze?›

~80% copper + ~20% tin.

What is stainless steel made of?›

Iron + carbon (0.03–0.8%) + chromium (16–18%) + nickel (10–14%) + molybdenum (2–3%).

Is an alloy a compound or a mixture?›

A mixture (homogeneous) — no new chemical bond forms between the different metal atoms.

What is solder, and what is it used for?›

An alloy of lead + tin. It has a low melting point, so it's used to weld electrical wires together.

What is an amalgam?›

An alloy in which one of the metals is mercury.

Why is jewellery made from 22-carat gold rather than 24-carat?›

Pure (24-carat) gold is very soft. Alloying it with silver or copper (22 parts gold : 2 parts Cu/Ag) makes it hard enough to wear.

How do an alloy's conductivity and melting point compare with the pure metal's?›

Both are lower. Brass and bronze conduct worse than copper, and solder melts lower than lead or tin.

Why is iron alloyed rather than used pure?›

Pure iron is very soft and stretches easily when hot. A little carbon makes it hard and strong. Adding nickel and chromium gives stainless steel, which doesn't rust.

Why are alloys usually harder than the pure metal?›

Atoms of a different size disrupt the neat layers, so the layers can't slide past each other as easily. beyond NCERT

Ionic Bonds & Ratios 25 cards
Define an ionic bond.›

The electrostatic force of attraction between oppositely charged ions that holds them together.

How is an ionic bond formed?›

By transfer of electrons, typically from a metal atom to a non-metal atom.

What happens to an atom with fewer than 4 valence electrons?›

It tends to donate/lose electrons, forming a positive ion (cation).

What happens to an atom with more than 4 valence electrons?›

It tends to gain or share electrons to complete its octet.

How does a sodium atom become Na⁺?›

It loses its one valence electron.

How does a chlorine atom become Cl⁻?›

It gains one electron to complete its octet.

Define a cation.›

A positively charged ion, formed when an atom loses electrons.

Define an anion.›

A negatively charged ion, formed when an atom gains electrons.

Do ionic compounds form molecules?›

No — they form 3-D crystal lattices, a repeating pattern of ions.

In the NaCl crystal lattice, how many Cl⁻ ions surround each Na⁺ ion?›

Six (and each Cl⁻ is surrounded by six Na⁺).

What order are ionic compounds named in?›

Cation name first, then anion name.

What ending do simple anion names usually have?›

“-ide” — e.g. chloride, oxide, sulfide.

In the criss-cross method, what do you write first?›

The cation symbol, then the anion symbol, with charges written underneath (not as superscripts).

Formula of calcium chloride (Ca²⁺ + Cl⁻)?›

CaCl₂

Formula of aluminium oxide (Al³⁺ + O²⁻)?›

Al₂O₃

Formula of magnesium hydroxide (Mg²⁺ + OH⁻)?›

Mg(OH)₂

When do you need brackets ( ) in an ionic formula?›

When 2 or more of the same polyatomic ion appear in the formula.

Do ionic compounds conduct electricity as solids?›

No — the ions are locked in the lattice and can't move.

When DO ionic compounds conduct electricity?›

When molten or dissolved in water — the ions become free to move.

Are ionic compounds generally soluble in water or in kerosene/petrol?›

Soluble in water; insoluble in kerosene and petrol.

Are melting/boiling points of ionic compounds high or low, and why?›

High — due to strong inter-ionic (electrostatic) attraction.

What rule decides the ratio of ions in an ionic compound?›

Total positive charge = total negative charge. The compound is electrically neutral overall.

Formula of sodium oxide (Na⁺ + O²⁻)?›

Na₂O. Two Na⁺ are needed to balance one O²⁻.

Formula of magnesium oxide (Mg²⁺ + O²⁻)?›

MgO. Criss-cross gives Mg₂O₂; simplify to the lowest ratio, 1 : 1.

Formula of ferric chloride (Fe³⁺ + Cl⁻)?›

FeCl₃

Polyatomic Ions 19 cards
Define a polyatomic ion.›

An ion formed from two or more atoms bonded together, carrying one overall charge.

Do polyatomic ion names usually end in “-ide”?›

No — unlike simple monoatomic anions.

Formula and valency of hydroxide?›

OH⁻, valency 1

Formula and valency of nitrate?›

NO₃⁻, valency 1

Formula and valency of hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate)?›

HCO₃⁻, valency 1

Formula and valency of carbonate?›

CO₃²⁻, valency 2

Formula and valency of sulfate?›

SO₄²⁻, valency 2

Formula and valency of ammonium?›

NH₄⁺, valency 1

Which is the only common polyatomic CATION in this list?›

Ammonium, NH₄⁺ — all the others are anions.

Inside a polyatomic ion, what holds the atoms together?›

Covalent bonds. The whole group carries one charge and forms ionic bonds with other ions.

Why write Ca(OH)₂ and not CaOH₂?›

CaOH₂ would mean 1 O and 2 H. The brackets show the whole OH⁻ unit is taken twice.

Formula of calcium carbonate (Ca²⁺ + CO₃²⁻)?›

CaCO₃. The 2+ and 2− balance 1 : 1, so no brackets are needed.

Formula of calcium nitrate (Ca²⁺ + NO₃⁻)?›

Ca(NO₃)₂

Formula of ammonium sulfate (NH₄⁺ + SO₄²⁻)?›

(NH₄)₂SO₄

Formula of aluminium sulfate (Al³⁺ + SO₄²⁻)?›

Al₂(SO₄)₃. 2 × (+3) = +6 balances 3 × (−2) = −6.

Formula of aluminium hydroxide (Al³⁺ + OH⁻)?›

Al(OH)₃

Formula of sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda)?›

NaHCO₃

Formula of ammonium chloride?›

NH₄Cl

Name these: Na₂SO₄ and K₂CO₃.›

Sodium sulfate; potassium carbonate.

Covalent Bonds 11 cards
Define a covalent bond.›

A bond formed by sharing a pair of electrons between two atoms. Each shared pair is one bond.

Between which kinds of elements do covalent bonds usually form?›

Non-metal + non-metal.

Why do two non-metals share electrons instead of transferring them?›

Both atoms attract electrons strongly and neither will give one up, so they share to complete their octets.

What is the octet rule?›

Atoms tend to lose, gain or share electrons until they have 8 electrons in their outer shell (a noble-gas configuration). Hydrogen aims for 2.

Single, double, triple bond: how many shared pairs? Give examples.›

1, 2 and 3 pairs. Single: H₂, Cl₂. Double: O₂. Triple: N₂.

How many electrons are shared in a triple bond?›

6 (three pairs), e.g. N≡N in N₂.

Do covalent compounds exist as molecules or as lattices?›

As separate molecules (H₂O, CO₂, CH₄), unlike the lattice of an ionic compound.

Are melting/boiling points of covalent compounds high or low? Why?›

Generally low. The forces between molecules are weak, even though the bonds inside each molecule are strong.

Do covalent compounds conduct electricity?›

Generally no. They have no ions or free electrons to carry charge (e.g. a sugar solution doesn't conduct).

Metallic vs ionic vs covalent: what are the electrons doing?›

Metallic: delocalized in a shared sea. Ionic: transferred. Covalent: shared between two specific atoms.

Is a polar covalent bond still a covalent bond?›

Yes. The electrons are still shared, just unequally.

Naming & Formulas with Prefixes 26 cards
Greek prefixes for 1 to 7?›

mono-1 · di-2 · tri-3 · tetra-4 · penta-5 · hexa-6 · hepta-7

Which prefix means 4? Which means 6?›

tetra- = 4 · hexa- = 6

Which prefix means 5? Which means 7?›

penta- = 5 · hepta- = 7

Which compounds are named with Greek prefixes: ionic or covalent?›

Covalent (non-metal + non-metal) only.

Why don't ionic compounds get prefixes? (MgCl₂ is not "magnesium dichloride")›

The ions' charges already fix the ratio, so the name doesn't need to state it. MgCl₂ = magnesium chloride.

Is mono- used on the first element?›

No. CO = carbon monoxide, not "monocarbon monoxide". Mono- is only used on the second element.

What ending does the second element get?›

-ide: oxide, chloride, sulfide, fluoride.

What is the vowel-drop rule?›

When a prefix ending in a or o comes before oxide, the vowel drops: mono + oxide → monoxide, penta + oxide → pentoxide, tetra + oxide → tetroxide.

What is the hydrogen rule?›

When hydrogen comes first, it gets no prefix: H₂S = hydrogen sulfide, not "dihydrogen sulfide".

Which compounds keep their common names?›

H₂O = water, NH₃ = ammonia.
