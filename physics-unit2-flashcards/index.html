Static friction 20 cards
Static friction formula›

fs ≤ μsN. It matches the push, up to a maximum of μsN.

Method: will it move?›

Find N, then fs,max = μsN. Compare with the applied force along the surface. Less: it stays put and fs = the push. More: it slides, and kinetic friction takes over.

Normal force with an angled push or pull›

Pull up at θ: N = mg − F sin θ. Push down at θ: N = mg + F sin θ. Only the horizontal part, F cos θ, moves it along.

Maximum acceleration of a car, runner or truck load›

amax = μsg. The mass cancels.

Static friction acts between two surfaces when›

they are not sliding relative to each other. Static means no sliding. Once the surfaces slide, friction becomes kinetic.

What does fs ≤ μsN mean?›

Static friction can be anything from zero up to a maximum of μsN. Static friction only pushes as hard as it needs to, to stop sliding. μsN is its limit, not its value.

A box sits on a level floor and nobody pushes it. What is the friction force on it?›

Zero. Nothing is trying to slide it, so static friction has nothing to oppose. Friction only appears when something pushes along the surface.

You push harder and harder on a heavy crate, but it still does not move. The static friction force on the crate›

increases to match your push. While the crate stays still the net force is zero, so friction equals your push. It grows with the push until it hits its maximum μsN.

A car speeds up on a level road without the tires skidding. The horizontal force that accelerates the car is›

static friction from the road on the tires, pointing forward. The tires push back on the road. By Newton's third law the road pushes the tires forward. The tire surface is not sliding on the road, so this is static friction.

When you walk forward, the friction force from the ground on your shoe points›

forward. Your foot pushes backward on the ground. The ground pushes forward on your foot (third law). That forward static friction is what moves you.

A crate rides on the flat bed of a truck that is speeding up. The crate does not slide. The friction force on the crate points›

forward, in the direction of the acceleration. Something must accelerate the crate forward. The only horizontal force on it is static friction, so friction points forward.

Why is it harder to start a heavy box moving than to keep it moving?›

μs is usually larger than μk. The largest static friction (μsN) is bigger than kinetic friction (μkN), so you need more force to break it free than to keep it sliding.

The coefficient of static friction depends on›

the two materials in contact. μ is a property of the pair of surfaces (rubber on concrete, wood on wood). It does not depend on area or weight. The friction force depends on weight through N.

What are the units of a coefficient of friction?›

It has no units. μ = f/N is a newton divided by a newton, so the units cancel.

A box rests on a ramp without sliding. The static friction on it points›

up the slope. Gravity's component mg sin θ tries to slide it down the slope. Static friction opposes that, so it points up the slope.

If the weight of a crate on a level floor is doubled (same surfaces), the maximum static friction force›

doubles. fs,max = μsN and N = mg on a level floor. Double the weight, double N, double the maximum friction.

You push a lawn mower handle down at an angle below the horizontal. Compared with pushing horizontally, the normal force from the ground is›

larger, so the maximum friction is larger. The downward part of your push (P sin θ) presses the mower into the ground: N = mg + P sin θ. Bigger N means bigger μN. That is why pulling up at an angle is easier than pushing down.

Anti-lock brakes stop a car in a shorter distance because they›

keep the tires rolling so static friction (larger) acts instead of kinetic friction. A skidding tire has kinetic friction. A rolling tire grips with static friction, and μs > μk, so the stopping force can be larger.

A graph shows the friction force on a box as the horizontal push increases from zero. Which description is correct?›

Friction rises equal to the push up to μsN, then drops to μkN and stays constant. Before sliding, static friction matches the push (a 45° line). At the peak μsN the box breaks free; then the smaller, constant kinetic friction takes over.

A book is held still against a vertical wall by a horizontal push. Which force holds the book up?›

Static friction from the wall, pointing up. The push and the normal force are horizontal. Only friction is vertical, so friction (up) balances the weight (down). The normal force sets the maximum friction μsN.

Kinetic friction 14 cards
Kinetic friction formula›

fk = μkN, opposite the sliding. Constant: it does not depend on speed or area.

Sliding to a stop on level ground (no push)›

a = μkg (backward). Then use kinematics: d = v²/(2μkg), t = v/(μkg). Mass cancels.

Constant velocity means…›

Net force = 0, so the push equals fk. If pushed horizontally on level ground: μk = P/(mg).

Static vs kinetic›

Usually μs > μk: harder to start it than to keep it going.

Kinetic friction acts when›

two surfaces slide past each other. Kinetic = moving. It acts only while the surfaces are sliding.

The direction of kinetic friction on a sliding object is›

opposite to the direction it slides. Friction opposes the relative motion of the surfaces. If a box slides right, kinetic friction on it points left, whatever the push is doing.

For a box sliding on a floor, how does the kinetic friction force change if the box slides faster?›

It stays about the same. In this course fk = μkN, which does not depend on speed (unlike air resistance).

A brick slides on its large face, then on its small end, with the same speed and the same surfaces. The kinetic friction force is›

the same in both cases. fk = μkN. The normal force is the brick's weight both ways and μ depends only on the materials, so area does not matter.

A crate is pushed across a floor at constant velocity. The push is›

equal to the kinetic friction force. Constant velocity means a = 0, so the net force is zero (Newton's first law). The push exactly balances kinetic friction.

A puck slides across a rough floor with no push and slows down. Which forces act on it?›

Weight, normal force and friction opposite the motion. Nothing pushes it forward once it is released. It keeps moving because of inertia, not a force. Friction alone slows it.

Two boxes, 5 kg and 20 kg, made of the same material, slide across the same floor with the same starting speed. Which slides farther before it stops?›

They slide the same distance. The deceleration is a = μkmg/m = μkg. The mass cancels, so both slow down at the same rate and stop in the same distance. The heavier box has more friction, but also more inertia.

In the formula fk = μkN, N is›

the normal force, which is not always equal to mg. N is the perpendicular contact force. It equals mg only on a level surface with no other vertical forces. Angled pushes and inclines change it.

A crate slides at constant velocity under a 150 N push. The push is increased to 200 N. What is the new net force on the crate?›

50 N forward. At constant velocity, kinetic friction = 150 N. Friction does not change when you push harder, so net force = 200 − 150 = 50 N.

Where does the kinetic energy of a sliding box go as friction stops it?›

It becomes thermal energy (the surfaces warm up). Friction converts kinetic energy into internal (thermal) energy. Energy is conserved; it just changes form.

Inclined planes 18 cards
Incline: the weight components›

Along the slope (down): mg sin θ. Into the slope: mg cos θ. "Sin to slide, cos to squeeze."

Incline: normal force›

N = mg cos θ (no other forces perpendicular to the slope).

Incline: acceleration›

Frictionless: a = g sin θ. Sliding down with friction: a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ). Sliding up: deceleration g(sin θ + μk cos θ).

Angle of repose / constant velocity›

Just about to slip: μs = tan θ. Sliding at constant velocity: μk = tan θ.

Incline: four steps›

1 Draw W, N, f. 2 Tilt the axes along and perpendicular to the slope. 3 Split W into mg sin θ and mg cos θ. 4 Perpendicular: N = mg cos θ; along: ΣF = ma.

On an incline at angle θ, the component of the weight that pulls the object down along the slope is›

mg sin θ. Sin for the slide, cos for the squeeze. Check the limits: at θ = 0 (flat) nothing pulls it along the surface, and sin 0 = 0.

For a block on an incline with no other forces pressing it into the surface, the normal force is›

mg cos θ. Perpendicular to the slope nothing accelerates, so N balances the "into the slope" component of the weight: N = mg cos θ, which is less than mg.

As the angle of an incline increases, the normal force on a block›

decreases. N = mg cos θ, and cos θ falls from 1 (flat) to 0 (vertical). The slope component mg sin θ rises at the same time.

A heavy block and a light block slide down the same frictionless ramp from rest. Which reaches the bottom first?›

They arrive together. a = g sin θ. The mass cancels, just as in free fall.

A block slides down an incline at constant velocity. What is μk?›

tan θ. Constant velocity means balanced forces along the slope: mg sin θ = μkmg cos θ, so μk = sin θ/cos θ = tan θ.

You slowly tilt a board until a block on it just begins to slip, at angle θ. What is μs?›

tan θ. At the point of slipping, mg sin θ = μsmg cos θ, so μs = tan θ. This angle is the angle of repose, and it is a quick lab method for μs.

A block is pushed up an incline and is still sliding upward. The kinetic friction on it points›

down the slope. Friction opposes the sliding, which is up the slope, so friction points down the slope, adding to mg sin θ. The block slows faster going up than it speeds up coming down.

Why do we tilt the axes to be parallel and perpendicular to the slope for incline problems?›

The acceleration is along the slope, so only the weight needs to be split into components. With tilted axes, N and friction already lie on the axes. Only mg is resolved (mg sin θ and mg cos θ), and the perpendicular equation gives N directly.

For a block on an incline, the angle between the weight vector and the line perpendicular to the slope is›

θ, the incline angle. The perpendicular to the slope is tilted from the vertical by the same angle the slope is tilted from the horizontal. That is why the "into the slope" part is mg cos θ.

A block sits at rest on an incline with μs = 0.80. The incline angle is 20°. The static friction force on the block equals›

mg sin 20°. Static friction is only as big as it needs to be. It balances the slope component of the weight: f = mg sin 20°. μsmg cos 20° is just the maximum it could provide.

What happens to the acceleration of a block sliding down a frictionless incline as the angle increases toward 90°?›

It increases toward g. a = g sin θ. At 90° the ramp is a vertical wall and the block is in free fall: a = g.

A block slides down a rough incline. Which equation gives its acceleration?›

a = g(sin θ − μk cos θ). Along the slope: mg sin θ − μkmg cos θ = ma. Divide by m. Going UP the slope it would be + (friction and gravity both point down the slope).

A block is placed at rest on an incline where tan θ is greater than μs. What happens?›

It slides down. It stays only if mg sin θ ≤ μsmg cos θ, that is tan θ ≤ μs. Here tan θ is bigger, so friction cannot hold it.

Universal gravitation 22 cards
Universal gravitation›

F = Gm₁m₂/r², G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², r center to center.

Field strength at distance r from a planet›

g = GM/r². At the surface r = R. Above it r = R + h.

Ratio shortcut›

New F = old F × (mass factors) ÷ (distance factor)². Double r → F/4. Triple r → F/9. Halve r → 4F.

Circular orbit›

GMm/r² = mv²/r → v = √(GM/r), T = 2πr/v, T² = 4π²r³/(GM). The satellite's mass cancels.

Earth data›

ME = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, RE = 6.38 × 10⁶ m, g = 9.81 m/s².

Newton's law of universal gravitation says the force between two masses is›

F = Gm₁m₂/r². Proportional to each mass and to the inverse square of the center-to-center distance. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg².

In F = Gm₁m₂/r², the distance r is measured›

between the centers of the two objects. Spheres act as if all their mass is at the center. For a satellite, r = Earth's radius + the altitude.

Compare the force Earth exerts on the Moon with the force the Moon exerts on Earth.›

They are equal in size and opposite in direction. They are a Newton's third law pair. Same size; the Moon just has much less mass, so it accelerates much more.

What is the difference between G and g?›

G is a universal constant; g is the gravitational acceleration at a particular place. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg² everywhere in the universe. g = GM/r² depends on the planet and how far you are from its center (9.81 m/s² at Earth's surface).

Who first measured the value of G, and how?›

Cavendish, with a torsion balance. Cavendish (1798) measured the tiny twist of a torsion balance as lead spheres attracted each other. That also let him "weigh the Earth".

Astronauts in the International Space Station float because›

they and the station are in free fall around Earth together. At ISS altitude g is still about 8.7 m/s² (89% of the surface value). Everything falls together, so nothing presses on anything: apparent weightlessness.

An astronaut has a mass of 70 kg on Earth. On the Moon, her mass is›

70 kg. Mass is the amount of matter and does not change. Her weight (mg) is about 1/6 as big on the Moon because g is smaller.

Why don't you notice the gravitational pull between you and the person next to you?›

G is so small that the force between everyday masses is tiny. Two 70 kg people 1 m apart attract with about 3 × 10⁻⁷ N, far too small to feel. Gravity is noticeable only when at least one mass is enormous.

A satellite moves in a circular orbit. What provides its centripetal force?›

Earth's gravitational pull. Gravity points toward Earth's center, which is the center of the circle: GMm/r² = mv²/r.

Two satellites orbit Earth at the same height. One has twice the mass of the other. Compared with the lighter one, the heavier satellite's orbital speed is›

the same. GMm/r² = mv²/r gives v = √(GM/r). The satellite's mass m cancels.

Compared with a satellite in a low orbit, a satellite in a higher orbit has›

a lower speed and a longer period. v = √(GM/r) decreases as r grows, and it also has farther to go: T = 2πr/v grows. The Moon (r = 384,000 km) takes 27.3 days.

Kepler's third law says that for planets orbiting the Sun›

T² is proportional to r³. From GMm/r² = m(2πr/T)²/r: T² = (4π²/GM)r³. Farther planets have much longer years.

What are the units of gravitational field strength g, and what are they equal to?›

N/kg, which equals m/s². g = F/m is force per unit mass (N/kg). Since 1 N = 1 kg·m/s², N/kg = m/s², the units of acceleration.

At what distance from Earth's CENTER is g one quarter of its surface value?›

2 Earth radii (one Earth radius above the surface). g ∝ 1/r². For g/4 you need r² four times bigger, so r = 2RE: one Earth radius above the surface.

The gravitational force between two objects is›

always attractive. Mass is always positive, so gravity always pulls. It also acts across empty space, with no contact needed.

A satellite in a circular orbit has an acceleration that›

points toward Earth's center. Its direction of motion keeps changing, so it is accelerating. The acceleration is centripetal, toward the center, and equals g at that height.

Which graph shape shows how the gravitational force F between two masses depends on the distance r between them?›

A curve that drops steeply and flattens out, never reaching zero (F ∝ 1/r²). Inverse-square: double r and F drops to ¼, triple r and F drops to 1/9. It gets small but never exactly zero.

Circular motion 22 cards
Centripetal acceleration›

ac = v²/r = 4π²r/T², toward the center. v = 2πr/T.

Centripetal force›

ΣFtoward center = mv²/r. It is a job done by a real force: tension, friction, gravity or normal. Never draw it as an extra arrow.

Vertical circle: top and bottom›

Top: T + mg = mv²/r. Bottom: T − mg = mv²/r. Minimum speed at the top: v = √(gr).

Hill and dip›

Top of a hill: N = mg − mv²/r (lighter; leaves the road at √(gr)). Bottom of a dip: N = mg + mv²/r (heavier).

Flat curve and banked curve›

Flat: vmax = √(μsgr). Banked, no friction: tan θ = v²/(rg).

An object moves in a circle at constant speed. The direction of its acceleration is›

toward the center of the circle. The speed is constant but the direction of the velocity keeps changing, so it accelerates. That centripetal acceleration points to the center: ac = v²/r.

At any instant, the velocity of an object moving in a circle points›

along the tangent to the circle. Velocity is tangent to the path; acceleration is toward the center. They are perpendicular, which is why the speed does not change.

A ball whirled in a horizontal circle on a string is released when the string breaks. It moves›

in a straight line along the tangent at the point of release. Once the inward force is gone, Newton's first law takes over: the ball keeps its velocity, which was tangent to the circle.

Which statement about the "centripetal force" is correct?›

It is the name for the net inward force, supplied by a real force such as tension, friction, gravity or the normal force. Never draw "Fc" on a free-body diagram. Draw the real forces, then set their inward total equal to mv²/r.

A car rounds a flat (unbanked) curve at constant speed. What force provides the centripetal force?›

Static friction from the road on the tires. The tires are not sliding sideways, so it is static friction, pointing toward the center of the curve. On ice there is too little friction and the car slides off along the tangent.

What provides the centripetal force that keeps the Moon in orbit?›

Earth's gravitational pull on the Moon. Gravity points toward Earth's center, the center of the orbit.

If the speed of an object moving in a circle is doubled (same radius), the centripetal force needed becomes›

4 times as large. Fc = mv²/r. Doubling v multiplies v² by 4. That is why fast curves are dangerous.

At the same speed, a car takes a curve with twice the radius. The centripetal force needed is›

half as large. Fc = mv²/r, so doubling r halves the force. Gentle curves need less friction.

In a car turning left, you feel pushed toward the right door. This is because›

your body tends to keep moving in a straight line (inertia) while the car turns. There is no outward force on you in the ground frame. Your body tries to go straight, the car turns under you, and the door pushes you inward to make you turn too.

A bucket of water is swung in a vertical circle. At the top, which forces act on the water?›

Gravity and the push of the bucket bottom, both downward. At the top both real forces point down, toward the center: N + mg = mv²/r. At the minimum speed N = 0 and gravity alone provides the centripetal force.

For a ball on a string swung in a vertical circle, the tension is greatest›

at the bottom. At the bottom T − mg = mv²/r, so T = mg + mv²/r: tension must hold up the weight and supply the centripetal force. At the top gravity helps, so T = mv²/r − mg.

A car drives over the top of a rounded hill. Compared with driving on flat ground, the normal force on the car is›

smaller. At the top the center is below the car: mg − N = mv²/r, so N = mg − mv²/r < mg. Riders feel lighter. At v = √(gr), N = 0 and the car leaves the road.

A car drives through the bottom of a dip in the road. The normal force on the car is›

larger than its weight. At the bottom the center is above: N − mg = mv²/r, so N = mg + mv²/r. Riders feel heavier.

On a banked curve at the design speed (no friction needed), what supplies the centripetal force?›

The horizontal component of the normal force. Tilting the road tilts N toward the center. N cos θ balances mg and N sin θ supplies mv²/r, giving tan θ = v²/(rg).

An object moves in a circle at constant speed. The work done by the centripetal force is zero because›

the force is always perpendicular to the velocity. A force perpendicular to the motion changes only the direction of the velocity, never the speed.

The period of circular motion is›

the time for one complete revolution. T in seconds. The number of revolutions per second is the frequency f = 1/T (in hertz).

In a spin cycle, water leaves the clothes through the holes in the drum because›

the drum cannot push on the water at the holes, so the water continues along the tangent. Where there is a hole there is no inward normal force, so nothing makes that water turn. It moves off in a straight line (tangent), out of the drum.

Newton's laws 19 cards
Newton's three laws›

1: no net force → constant velocity. 2: Fnet = ma. 3: A on B = −(B on A), different objects, same type.

Elevator (apparent weight)›

N = m(g + a) accelerating up; N = m(g − a) accelerating down; N = mg at constant velocity; N = 0 in free fall.

Connected objects›

Treat the whole system for a (internal tensions cancel), then one object alone for the tension or contact force.

Free-body diagram checklist›

One dot, real forces only (W, N, T, f, applied), each from a named object. Arrow lengths to scale. No "Fc", no "force of motion".

Newton's first law says that an object with zero net force›

stays at rest or keeps moving at constant velocity. No net force means no change in velocity. Motion does not need a force; changes in motion do.

Inertia is measured by an object's›

mass. More mass means more resistance to changes in motion. Weight is the gravitational force, which changes from planet to planet; mass does not.

A hockey puck slides across frictionless ice at constant velocity. The net force on it is›

zero. Constant velocity means a = 0, so Fnet = 0. It keeps moving by inertia.

The same net force acts on a 2 kg cart and a 6 kg cart. Compared with the 2 kg cart, the 6 kg cart's acceleration is›

one third as large. a = F/m. Three times the mass gives one third the acceleration.

Newton's third law force pairs›

act on two different objects and are equal and opposite. A pushes B, B pushes A: same size, opposite direction, same type of force, different objects. Because they act on different objects they never cancel.

A book rests on a table. The reaction force to the Earth's gravitational pull on the book is›

the book's gravitational pull on the Earth. Swap the objects: "Earth pulls book" becomes "book pulls Earth". The normal force balances the weight, but it is not its third-law pair (different type, same object).

You stand on a scale in an elevator that is accelerating upward. The scale reads›

more than your weight. N − mg = ma with a up, so N = m(g + a) > mg. The scale reads N.

An elevator moves upward at constant velocity. Compared with your weight, the scale reading is›

equal. Constant velocity means a = 0, so N = mg, whichever way it moves.

A skydiver falls at terminal velocity. Which statement is true?›

Air resistance equals her weight, so the net force is zero. At terminal velocity she no longer speeds up, so a = 0 and the forces balance.

A rocket accelerates in empty space because›

it pushes exhaust gas backward, and the gas pushes the rocket forward. Newton's third law: rocket pushes gas back, gas pushes rocket forward. No air is needed.

A horse pulls a cart and they accelerate forward. What makes the horse (and the cart) speed up?›

The ground pushing forward on the horse's hooves is larger than the cart pulling back on the horse. Horse and cart pull each other equally (third law). The horse speeds up because the forward friction from the ground on its hooves beats the cart's backward pull.

A truck and a small car collide head-on. During the collision, the force on the car is›

equal in size to the force on the truck. Third law: equal and opposite. The car has less mass, so the same force gives it a larger acceleration (a = F/m), which is why it is damaged more.

You pull a box across the floor with a rope angled upward. Compared with the box's weight, the normal force is›

less. The upward part of the pull (F sin θ) supports some of the weight: N = mg − F sin θ.

In free fall (an elevator whose cable has snapped), the scale under your feet would read›

zero. With a = g downward, N = m(g − a) = 0. You and the scale fall together: apparent weightlessness, like the astronauts in the ISS.

Which pair of forces is a Newton's third law pair?›

Your foot pushes back on the ground; the ground pushes forward on your foot. Third-law pairs always involve exactly two objects that swap places. The other options are pairs of forces on ONE object.
