VSEPR & electron domains 11 cards
What does VSEPR stand for?›

Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion.

What is the main idea of VSEPR theory?›

Electron pairs around the central atom repel each other, so they spread out as far apart as possible. That arrangement sets the shape of the molecule (NCERT §4.4).

What is an electron domain?›

A region of electron density around the central atom. Each bonded atom counts as 1 (single, double or triple bond), and each lone pair on the central atom counts as 1.

How many electron domains does a double or triple bond count as?›

One. NCERT: a multiple bond is treated like a single "super pair".

Do the lone pairs on the outer atoms count as domains?›

No. Only the lone pairs on the central atom affect the shape.

How many electron domains are around C in CO₂?›

2: two double bonds, and each counts once.

How many electron domains are around N in NH₃?›

4: 3 bonded H atoms + 1 lone pair.

How many electron domains are around S in SO₂?›

3: 2 bonded O atoms + 1 lone pair.

Does resonance change the shape?›

No. VSEPR works on any one resonance structure, and they all give the same number of domains (NCERT §4.4). HCO₂⁻ and NO₂⁻ each have 2 resonance forms and 1 shape.

Rank the three kinds of electron-pair repulsion.›

lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair.

Why does a lone pair repel more than a bonding pair?›

A bonding pair is held between two nuclei. A lone pair belongs only to the central atom, so it spreads out and takes more space.

Electron geometry 7 cards
2 electron domains: which electron geometry, and what angle?›

Linear, 180°.

3 electron domains: which electron geometry, and what angle?›

Trigonal planar, 120° (360° ÷ 3, all in one plane).

4 electron domains: which electron geometry, and what angle?›

Tetrahedral, 109.5°.

Why is the tetrahedral angle 109.5° and not 90°?›

In 3-D, four pairs can spread farther than a flat "+". Pointing them to alternate corners of a cube gives cos θ = (1 − 1 − 1)/3 = −1/3, so θ = 109.5°.

What is the difference between electron geometry and molecular geometry?›

Electron geometry includes every domain, lone pairs too. Molecular geometry describes only where the atoms are.

When are the electron geometry and the molecular geometry the same?›

When the central atom has no lone pairs.

5 and 6 electron domains?beyond the test›

Trigonal bipyramidal (90° and 120°) and octahedral (90°), as in PCl₅ and SF₆. They need an expanded octet.

Molecular geometry & bond angles 10 cards
2 bonded atoms, 0 lone pairs?›

Linear, 180°: CO₂, HCN.

3 bonded atoms, 0 lone pairs?›

Trigonal planar, 120°: BF₃, SO₃, HCO₂⁻.

2 bonded atoms, 1 lone pair (3 domains)?›

Bent, slightly less than 120°: SO₂ (119.5°), NO₂⁻, NOCl.

4 bonded atoms, 0 lone pairs?›

Tetrahedral, 109.5°: CH₄, CCl₄, CF₂Cl₂.

3 bonded atoms, 1 lone pair (4 domains)?›

Trigonal pyramidal, ≈107° (less than 109.5°): NH₃, PBr₃.

2 bonded atoms, 2 lone pairs (4 domains)?›

Bent, ≈104.5° (less than 109.5°): H₂O.

Why is the angle in NH₃ 107° and not 109.5°?›

The lone pair on N repels more strongly than the bonding pairs, so it squeezes the H–N–H angle below 109.5° (NCERT Table 4.8).

Why is the angle in H₂O (104.5°) smaller than in NH₃ (107°)?›

Water has two lone pairs, and lone pair–lone pair repulsion is the strongest, so the bonds are squeezed further.

"Bent" appears twice in the VSEPR chart. What are the two cases?›

3 domains with 1 lone pair (less than 120°, e.g. SO₂), and 4 domains with 2 lone pairs (≈104.5°, e.g. H₂O). Write the electron geometry too, so it is clear which one you mean.

What does the label AX₃E mean?›

A = the central atom, X₃ = 3 bonded atoms, E = 1 lone pair. That is trigonal pyramidal, e.g. NH₃ (NCERT Table 4.7).

3-D redraw 6 cards
In a 3-D drawing, what does a plain line mean?›

The bond lies in the plane of the page.

What does a solid wedge mean?›

The bond comes out of the page, toward you.

What does a hashed wedge (a dash) mean?›

The bond goes back, away from you.

How do you draw a tetrahedral molecule such as CF₂Cl₂?›

2 plain lines in the page, 1 wedge and 1 dash. In a tetrahedron every corner is equivalent, so it does not matter which atom goes where.

How do you draw a trigonal pyramidal molecule such as PBr₃?›

The lone pair on top of P; below it, 1 plain line, 1 wedge and 1 dash to the three Br atoms.

Which shapes need no wedges or dashes?›

Linear, trigonal planar and bent: all their atoms lie in one plane.

Bond polarity 8 cards
When is a covalent bond polar?›

When the two atoms have different electronegativities. The more electronegative atom pulls the shared pair closer and becomes δ−; the other becomes δ+.

What is the ΔEN rule for bonds (from C1)?›

ΔEN less than 0.5: nonpolar covalent · 0.5 to 1.7: polar covalent · more than 1.7: mostly ionic.

Is a C–H bond polar?›

ΔEN = 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4, so it is nonpolar.

Is a P–Br bond polar?›

ΔEN = 2.8 − 2.1 = 0.7, so it is polar, with Br δ−.

Is a C–F bond polar?›

ΔEN = 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5, so it is polar.

Is an N–Cl bond polar?›

ΔEN = 3.0 − 3.0 = 0, so it is nonpolar (with NCERT values).

What is a dipole moment?›

µ = Q × r: charge times the distance between the centres of + and − charge. Its unit is the debye: 1 D = 3.33564 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. It is a vector (NCERT §4.3.6).

How is a bond dipole drawn?›

As a crossed arrow: the cross (+) at the δ+ end and the arrowhead at the δ− end. It points toward the more electronegative atom.

Molecular polarity 10 cards
What decides whether a whole molecule is polar?›

The vector sum of its bond dipoles (NCERT §4.3.6). If they cancel, it is nonpolar; if not, it is polar. So the shape decides.

Can a molecule with polar bonds be nonpolar?›

Yes, if the shape is symmetric so the dipoles cancel: CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄.

Why is CO₂ nonpolar even though C=O is polar?›

It is linear, so the two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions: µ − µ = 0 (Table 4.5: 0 D).

Why is H₂O polar?›

It is bent, so the two O–H dipoles add instead of cancelling. The net is 2µ cos(104.5°/2) = 1.85 D.

BF₃ or NF₃: which is polar?›

NF₃. It is trigonal pyramidal, with a lone pair. BF₃ is trigonal planar and symmetric, so its dipoles cancel.

CCl₄ is nonpolar but CHCl₃ is polar. Why?›

Swapping one Cl for H breaks the symmetry, so the dipoles no longer cancel: 0 D vs 1.04 D (NCERT Table 4.5).

The three-question polarity test?›

1. Any polar bonds? If not, it is nonpolar. 2. Any lone pairs on the central atom? If so, it is polar. 3. Are all the outer atoms identical? If so, it is nonpolar; if not, it is polar.

Is HCN polar?›

Yes. It is linear, but the two ends are different (C–H is nonpolar and C≡N is polar), so nothing cancels the C≡N dipole.

Is CH₄ polar?›

No. Its C–H bonds are nonpolar (ΔEN 0.4), and it is symmetric anyway.

Can an ion like NO₂⁻ be "polar"?›

Yes. Use the same test: NO₂⁻ is bent, so its bond dipoles don't cancel, and it is polar. CO₃²⁻ and NH₄⁺ are symmetric, so they are nonpolar.

HCO₂⁻ and NO₂⁻ both have 18 electrons. Why are their shapes different?›

NO₂⁻ has a lone pair on N (3 domains, 2 atoms), so it is bent. HCO₂⁻ has none on C (3 domains, 3 atoms), so it is trigonal planar. Count the domains every time.

In NOCl, why does the double bond go to O and not to Cl?›

With N=O every formal charge is 0. A Cl=N bond would put +1 on Cl and −1 on O, which is worse.
